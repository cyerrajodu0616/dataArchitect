Distance Metrics: Cosine, Dot Product, Euclidean

Week 1, Day 2 — how "closeness" gets defined in vector space, and why it matters for retrieval quality

Yesterday you established that an embedding is a vector representation of meaning — a point in high-dimensional space. A vector by itself is useless. What makes it useful is a way to measure how close two vectors are. That's what a distance metric (or its inverse, a similarity metric) does. Pick the wrong one and your retrieval quietly degrades — no error, no exception, just worse results that are hard to diagnose.

The three you'll actually encounter

1. Euclidean distance (L2)

Straight-line distance between two points — the Pythagorean theorem generalized to N dimensions. For vectors a and b: 

d(a, b) = sqrt( Σ (a_i − b_i)² )

Smaller = closer. This is the metric your intuition already has from geometry class. It's sensitive to both direction and magnitude — a vector twice as long, pointing the same way, registers as far away.

2. Dot product

a · b = Σ (a_i × b_i)

Larger = more similar (it's a similarity, not a distance). It rewards vectors that point the same way and have large magnitude — a vector's own length inflates its dot product with everything else, including itself. That magnitude-sensitivity is the whole story with dot product: it's cheap to compute (one multiply-accumulate pass, no square root), but it conflates "similar direction" with "large magnitude," which is a problem if magnitude in your embedding space carries no real semantic content.

3. Cosine similarity

cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)

This is the dot product, normalized by both vectors' lengths. What's left is purely the angle between them — magnitude is divided out entirely. Range is −1 (opposite) to 1 (identical direction). Because it isolates direction, cosine answers a narrower, more useful question: "do these two vectors point the same way in meaning-space?" — regardless of how long either one happens to be.

Worked example
Take two product-description embeddings, a and b, where b happens to be exactly 2 × a (same direction, doubled magnitude).
  • Euclidean distance: large — treats them as far apart, even though they point identically.
  • Dot product: a·b = a·(2a) = 2(a·a) — doubled, purely from b's length. Looks "more similar" for the wrong reason.
  • Cosine: cos(a,b) = 1.0 — correctly identical, because the normalization cancels the magnitude difference out.
Cosine is the only one of the three that isn't fooled by this.

Why magnitude is noise, not signal, in most text embeddings

In a lot of embedding models, a vector's magnitude ends up correlated with things you don't want driving your retrieval — like how long or generic the source text was — rather than with how strongly it expresses a concept. Direction, not length, is where the semantic content lives. That's a modeling choice baked in at training time: models like OpenAI's text-embedding-3 family are trained with a contrastive objective that explicitly optimizes cosine similarity between related pairs and pushes it down for unrelated pairs. The model is told during training that angle is what matters. Using a different metric at inference time than the one the model was optimized for throws away that training signal.

One practical wrinkle: OpenAI's embeddings are returned pre-normalized to unit length (‖v‖ = 1). When every vector has the same magnitude, dot product and cosine become mathematically identical — dividing by 1 × 1 does nothing. This is why you'll see pgvector examples using the <#> (negative inner product) operator against OpenAI embeddings and calling it "cosine similarity" — for these specific vectors, that's a true statement, and dot product is slightly cheaper to compute since it skips the normalization step. But that shortcut only holds because OpenAI normalized on your behalf. If you ever mix in vectors that aren't unit-normalized — a different model, a manually-averaged vector, a raw pooled hidden state — dot product and cosine diverge again, and dot product silently starts rewarding magnitude.

Retail example
Product catalog search
Two product description embeddings can point in nearly the same direction (same product concept) while one description is three sentences and the other is thirty words padded with SEO keywords. If you retrieved with raw dot product on non-normalized vectors, the longer, keyword-stuffed description could win on magnitude alone — semantically weaker but numerically "bigger." Cosine strips that out and ranks on meaning.

Your Elasticsearch experience is the right anchor here — and also the contrast

You've done entity resolution with Elasticsearch trigram search at ~100M rows. That's worth placing side by side with cosine, because it clarifies what's actually different about semantic retrieval — the distinction isn't really about the metric formula, it's about what space you're measuring distance in.

​ Trigram / edit-distance family Cosine on embeddings
What space Character n-grams / edit operations Learned semantic space
Catches Typos, near-duplicate strings, prefix/suffix overlap Synonyms, paraphrase, conceptual similarity
Misses "sneakers" vs "trainers" — zero literal overlap, same meaning Exact SKU / model-number discrimination — "iPhone 15" vs "iPhone 15 Pro" can land close in meaning-space despite being different SKUs
Entity-resolution role Great for "same entity, different spelling/typo" Great for "same intent, different words" — weaker on exact identifiers

This is exactly why Week 3 (RAG architecture) has a day on hybrid retrieval — BM25/trigram-style lexical matching plus embedding cosine similarity, run in parallel and merged. At 100M-row scale, you already know the operational cost of lexical matching (index size, query latency). The architect-level judgment call is: lexical catches what vector search structurally can't (exact SKU codes, part numbers, typos), and vector search catches what lexical structurally can't (synonyms, intent). Neither replaces the other — that's the "why hybrid over either alone" argument you'll want ready for interviews.

Decision rule

When to use which
  • Cosine — default for text embeddings, always, unless you have a specific reason not to. This is what the model was trained against.
  • Dot product — fine as a drop-in for cosine only if your vectors are guaranteed unit-normalized (true for OpenAI's embeddings). Slight compute saving, same ranking.
  • Euclidean — rare for text. Shows up more in non-text embedding spaces (e.g. image feature vectors, clustering algorithms like k-means that are defined in terms of Euclidean distance) where magnitude is meaningful.

Deep dive

Going one level further: the arithmetic by hand, what the index is actually doing with these metrics, and where this breaks in production.

1. Worked example by hand — where dot product and cosine actually disagree

The earlier example (b = 2a) showed dot product inflating a same-direction pair. That's the easy case. The more dangerous case is when dot product and cosine disagree on the ranking — not just the score, the actual order of "most similar." Here's a minimal example you can check with a calculator.

Let a = (3, 4), so ‖a‖ = 5. Two candidates:

  • Y = (3, 4) — identical direction and magnitude to a. Perfect match.
  • X = (2.4, 19.9) — magnitude 20, but rotated 30° away from a's direction.
dot(a, Y) = 3(3) + 4(4)      = 25
dot(a, X) = 3(2.4) + 4(19.9) ≈ 86.8

cos(a, Y) = 25 / (5 × 5)     = 1.000   ← perfect direction match
cos(a, X) = 86.8 / (5 × 20)  ≈ 0.868   ← 30° off

By dot product, X beats Y more than 3-to-1 (86.8 vs 25) — it would rank first. By cosine, Y is the better match (1.000 vs 0.868), correctly, because it's the one actually pointing the same way. Dot product let X's sheer size overrule the fact that it's the worse directional match. This is the failure mode in miniature — at embedding scale (1536 dimensions instead of 2), it's invisible in a debugger; it just shows up as "retrieval sometimes returns weird stuff" with no obvious cause.

Y overlaps a's direction exactly; X is rotated away but much longer. Dot product prefers X. Cosine correctly prefers Y.

2. What the index is actually doing with your chosen metric

Choosing a metric isn't just a query-time decision — pgvector builds the index around it, using one of two ANN (approximate nearest neighbor) algorithms:

IVFFlat — at build time, it runs k-means clustering on your vectors to carve the space into lists partitions (Voronoi cells), using your chosen metric as the clustering distance. At query time, it finds the probes nearest cluster centroids to your query vector, then does an exact distance scan only within those clusters. Fewer probes = faster but more likely to miss the true nearest neighbor sitting just across a cluster boundary.

HNSW — builds a multi-layer graph where each vector is a node connected to its approximate nearest neighbors (again, by your chosen metric). Search starts at a sparse top layer and greedily walks toward the query vector, dropping down a layer each time it can't get closer, until it reaches the bottom, densest layer. No clustering step, generally better recall/speed tradeoff than IVFFlat, at the cost of more expensive index builds and more memory.

The gotcha that matters at your scale
In pgvector, the index is built with an explicit operator class — vector_cosine_ops, vector_l2_ops, or vector_ip_ops. If that operator class doesn't match the operator in your query's ORDER BY clause (<=>, <->, <#> respectively), Postgres silently ignores the index and falls back to a full sequential scan. No error. Correct results, wrong performance profile. At small data volumes you won't notice. At 100M rows — exactly the scale you've operated at with Elasticsearch — this is the difference between a 50ms query and a query that times out. This is precisely the class of bug an experienced data engineer catches by habit (running EXPLAIN ANALYZE, checking for Index Scan vs Seq Scan in the plan) that a GenAI-only engineer often doesn't think to check.

3. Failure modes worth knowing before they bite you

  • Operator/index mismatch (above) — the most common silent production bug.
  • Mixed normalization — if you ever blend vectors from different sources (a fine-tuned model, a manually pooled hidden state, a different provider) into the same index without verifying they're all unit-normalized, cosine distance is still safe (it normalizes per-pair at query time) but <#> silently stops being equivalent to cosine and starts rewarding whichever source happens to produce larger-magnitude vectors.
  • Concentration of measure in high dimensions — with hundreds or thousands of dimensions, pairwise cosine similarities for unrelated items tend to compress into a narrower band than intuition from 2D/3D suggests. A cosine score of 0.75 can mean very different things depending on the embedding model and dimensionality — it's not a portable, calibrated "percent similar."
  • Fixed similarity thresholds don't transfer across models — "only return results above cosine 0.8" tuned for one embedding model will silently misbehave if you swap embedding models or providers later, since the score distribution shifts. Threshold tuning is per-model, not universal — this is part of why Week 3's RAG eval day (RAGAS) matters: measuring retrieval quality empirically beats trusting a raw cosine cutoff.

Real run — the actual numbers

The worked example above was hypothetical. Here's what text-embedding-3-small actually returned for the 3 sentences:

pair cosine dot euclidean
A,B — paraphrases 0.632 0.632 0.858
A,C — unrelated 0.155 0.155 1.299
B,C — unrelated 0.161 0.161 1.295

Two things worth internalizing here. First, cosine and dot product agree to 3 decimals on every row — the unit-normalization claim from earlier in this lesson, now confirmed empirically instead of just asserted. Second: the paraphrase pair only scored 0.632, not the 0.75–0.90 you might expect for "same meaning, different words." Absolute cosine values are less informative than the gap between related and unrelated pairs — 0.632 vs ~0.16 is a clean ~4x separation, and that separation, not the raw number, is the actual retrieval signal. Don't eyeball a single cosine score and judge it "high" or "low" in isolation; judge it against the score for something you know is unrelated.

Visualize it

Numbers in a table are convincing, but a plot makes the "direction is what matters" idea visceral. A 3-sentence example projects cleanly to 2D: reduce the 1536-dimension vectors down using PCA and MDS, plot each sentence as a vector from the origin, and look at how far apart the arrowheads land.

PCA vs MDS — pick the one that matches your question
These are not interchangeable projections. PCA finds the 2 directions that capture the most variance across your whole dataset — it doesn't explicitly try to preserve pairwise distances, so a PCA plot's dot-to-dot distances are a byproduct, not a guarantee. MDS is built differently: its objective function directly minimizes distortion of pairwise distances when squashing into 2D. If the question you're asking the plot is "does closer-together mean more similar?" — MDS is the one actually optimized to answer that; PCA is answering a different question ("where's the variance?") that often, but not always, correlates with the same answer.

What happens when the model changes underneath you

Worth stating plainly, since it's the kind of thing that bites in production and not in a demo: old-model and new-model embeddings are not comparable, even at the same dimensionality — a model upgrade is a different coordinate system, not a drop-in replacement. Swapping models under a live index without a full re-embed produces a silently mixed-version index: relevance degrades inconsistently depending which document a query happens to land near, with no error raised anywhere. The correct move is a full backfill into a new index, quality-validated (RAGAS, Week 3) before cutover, with the model name/version stored as a column on every vector row so a partially-migrated index is detectable rather than invisible. File this next to the operator-class mismatch from earlier — same shape of bug, different cause.

Apply it to your agent

pgvector supports all three as index/query operators: <-> (L2), <#> (negative inner product), <=> (cosine distance). Open your agent's retrieval query and check which operator you're actually using. If it's <=>, you're already doing the right thing by default. If it's <#>, confirm your embeddings are unit-normalized (OpenAI's are) — worth stating explicitly in your justification doc as a deliberate choice, not an accident.
