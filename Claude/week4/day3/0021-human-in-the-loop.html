Human in the Loop — Where the Gate Goes, and Why

Week 4, Day 3 — the approval threshold is arithmetic, and "review everything" is the trap

The question, in plain words

Your agent can now do things — issue a refund, approve a policy, resolve a dispute. Some of those it will get wrong.

So: when should it stop and ask a person first?

Almost every team answers this by picking a round number in a meeting. Usually $500. It's rarely revisited, and it's almost never right — because it's an arithmetic problem, and the arithmetic has a surprise in it.

1. The Obvious Half

Two costs, pulling opposite ways:

What it costs
Reviewing an actionA person's minutes. At $45/hour loaded, a 3-minute review is $2.25.
Not reviewing itP(the agent is wrong) × what wrong costs. At a 4% error rate on a $42 refund, that's $1.68.
review it when   P(wrong) × cost_if_wrong   >   cost_to_review

Rearrange and you get a dollar threshold directly: threshold = review_cost / error_rate. For retail that's $2.25 / 0.04 = $56. Review anything above $56, let the rest through.

That's the textbook answer. It's also wrong, and the reason is interesting.

2. The Half That Changes the Answer

In plain words

If you give a person five things to check in an hour, they read them properly.

If you give the same person forty things to check in an hour, they click approve without reading.

Reviewer attention is finite. So the catch rate isn't a fixed number — it falls as the queue grows. And that single fact turns the whole problem upside down.

You've seen this exact failure before
It's alert fatigue. Every team that has ever built a monitoring system has learned that a channel with 400 alerts a day is functionally the same as a channel with none — except that it costs money and creates the belief that someone is watching.

An approval queue is an alert channel where each alert has a button on it. The failure mode is identical, and so is the fix: send fewer, higher-signal items.

The consequence
Total cost is U-shaped in the threshold:

Threshold too high → bad actions go through unreviewed.
Threshold too low → the queue floods, reviewers rubber-stamp, and you pay full price for a review that caught nothing.

The finding to carry into an interview
"Review everything" is not the safe default. Two reviewers can physically process 6,400 items a month. Route them 120,000 and 113,600 are auto-approved on a timeout — and the 6,400 that are seen arrive at 40/hour, where the catch rate has already fallen to 25%.

And the failure is invisible: the approvals all come back signed. Every dashboard shows 100% of high-value actions reviewed. It looks like the strongest possible control right up until someone audits what the reviewers actually caught. This is an expensive way to convert a real control into a theatrical one.

4. Sometimes Staffing Is the Threshold

Run the insurance case and something different happens:

python approval_economics.py --domain insurance Cheapest gate : $16,937 Reviewed : 1,099/month (45.8% of actions) Catch rate there : 92% (6.9 items/hour across 3 reviewers) THE OPTIMUM IS SET BY STAFFING, NOT BY VALUE. At $16,937 the queue is 1,099/month against a capacity of 1,152. Lower the threshold by one step and the queue overflows — the excess gets approved on a timeout with no scrutiny at all.
The threshold isn't really the decision
The optimum sits exactly at the capacity line: 1,099 routed against 1,152 reviewable. One step lower and the queue overflows.

So the real question is not "what's the right threshold?" — it's "how many reviewers do we have?", and the threshold is whatever keeps the queue inside that. Staffing is the control. The threshold is just how you express it.

That reframing is worth having ready. "We'd set it at $500" is a guess. "We have three underwriters, that's 1,150 reviews a month, so the threshold is whatever routes 1,150 cases — and today that's about $17k" is a capacity plan.

5. The Four Shapes of Human Involvement

"Human in the loop" is four different things, and they have very different costs:

PatternWhat happensCostUse when
Approve / reject Agent pauses before acting. Human says yes or no. Highest — blocks the action, adds full queue latency. Irreversible and expensive. Money leaving, a policy bound.
Edit then approve Human can change the agent's draft before it goes. Highest, plus more time per item. The agent is usually right about what and often wrong about details — a letter, a coverage recommendation.
Escalate only Agent acts alone; hands over only when it's unsure. Low — only the hard cases queue. The default. Needs a calibrated confidence signal, which is the hard part.
Review after the fact Agent acts. A sample is audited later. Lowest — zero latency, no blocking. Reversible actions. Catches systematic drift, which per-item review is bad at.
The one people skip — and it catches what the others can't
Review-after-the-fact is not a weaker version of approval. It answers a different question. Per-item approval asks "is this one right?" Sampled audit asks "has the agent started being wrong in a new way?"

A reviewer approving items one at a time will not notice that the error rate moved from 4% to 11% last Tuesday. A weekly audit of 50 sampled actions will. Most systems need both, and the audit is far cheaper — it's the same golden-set discipline as Week 3 Day 4, applied to actions instead of retrieval.

6. How a Pause Actually Works

Why pausing is technically hard

"Wait for a human" sounds easy. It isn't, because the wait may be three days, and no process stays alive for three days.

So the agent has to: save everything it knows, exit completely, and later — in a different process, on a different machine, possibly after a deploy — pick up at exactly the node it stopped at, with the same state.

# LangGraph: stop before a node, resume later by thread id app = graph.compile( checkpointer=PostgresSaver(conn), # state saved after every node interrupt_before=["execute_refund"], # stop here, always ) # turn 1 — runs until it hits the gate, then returns app.invoke({"request": msg}, config={"configurable": {"thread_id": case_id}}) # ... three days pass. The pod restarts twice. There is a deploy. # turn 2 — resumes at execute_refund with the state it had app.invoke(None, config={"configurable": {"thread_id": case_id}})
The thing to notice
thread_id is doing all the work — it's the primary key of a durable row holding the whole state. Resuming is a SELECT, not magic.

Which is exactly why Day 2's damage table scored "pause for a human" and "survive a crash" identically: they're the same requirement. Both need durable state resumable at an exact node. Day 5 sizes what that costs in Postgres.

7. Three Domains, Three Different Gates

Retail — refunds

120k actions/mo · $42 mean
optimum: $170 · 1.5% reviewed

Escalate-only + sampled audit. The arithmetic says review almost nothing, and that unintuitive answer is correct — a $42 refund isn't worth three minutes at a 4% error rate.

Note the gate barely pays for itself: $190k vs $202k reviewing nothing. The bigger lever here is the error rate, not the gate.

Insurance — underwriting

2,400 actions/mo · $18k mean
optimum: $17k · capacity-bound

Approve/reject, and a regulatory floor underneath. Some decisions must be reviewed whether or not the maths agrees.

The threshold is the lower of the economic answer and the compliance answer — and here compliance binds first. The economics then tell you what that requirement costs, which is still worth knowing.

Fintech — disputes

45k actions/mo · $260 mean
optimum: $465 · 39-day queue

Escalate-only, and the queue length is the binding problem. 39 days to clear against a network deadline measured in days.

Here a slow queue isn't a cost — it's an automatic loss. The deadline passes and the dispute resolves against you. Latency is the constraint, not money.

8. What This Model Does Not Price

Be straight about the limits — an interviewer will probe them, and having the list ready is worth more than the model.

Not pricedWhy it matters
Regulatory floors Some decisions must be reviewed regardless of expected value. The real threshold is the lower of the economic and compliance answers. The model's job then shifts: it tells you what the requirement costs, not whether to have it.
Correlated failure This prices one error at a time. A bug that makes the agent wrong the same way 4,000 times isn't 4,000 small losses — it's one incident. That needs a circuit breaker, which is Day 4, not a threshold.
Accountability Some gates exist to create an accountable human, not to catch errors. That's legitimate and the model can't see it. Say so rather than pretending the arithmetic settles it.
Reviewer learning Reviewers who see only hard cases get better at them; reviewers who see everything get worse at all of them. The fatigue curve here is static, and the real one probably steepens over months.
