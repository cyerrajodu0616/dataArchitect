The Week 4 checkpoint is: diagram your agent's state graph and justify each edge. The diagram is the easy half. The justification is the point — and every edge below traces to a number from Days 1–5.

1. The Graph

2. ADR-004

ACCEPTED   ADR-004: Agent orchestration — framework, control flow, and durable state

Context

ADR-002 chose pgvector; ADR-003 designed the retrieval pipeline. Neither says how the agent around them is structured. In practice that had defaulted to a linear chain: classify, look up, check policy, act. It works until the first ambiguous message, at which point it guesses — and we measured it guessing a refund for a customer who wanted a tracking number.

Three requirements forced this ADR. Ambiguous input needs a clarification loop, which a chain cannot express. Refunds above a threshold need human approval, which needs a pause of hours. And runs must survive a restart, because an underwriting case can be two weeks long.

Decision

  1. State machine, not a chain. LangGraph, with explicit nodes and conditional edges.
  2. Six nodes, deliberately few. Under the independent required-step model, additional nodes cost reliability multiplicatively; full snapshots of linearly growing state cost storage quadratically.
  3. One clarification loop, exit after 2 questions to a human handoff. The step limit is a backstop, not the exit condition.
  4. Human approval above $170, derived from review cost, error rate and reviewer capacity — not chosen in a meeting.
  5. Idempotency keys on every side-effecting call, shipped before retries, plus exponential backoff with jitter and a circuit breaker on error rate.
  6. Postgres checkpointing on the ADR-002 instance, delta format, state trimmed of raw tool payloads, partitioned by created_at.

Consequences

Accepted costs. A framework dependency with a release cadence and abstractions the team must eventually read the source of. A review queue at ~1,818 items/month needing 2 reviewers. 29 GB/month of checkpoint storage and 12 writes/sec of additional load on the OLTP instance — 0.5% of existing write traffic.

Accepted risks. The $170 threshold rests on an assumed 4% agent error rate that we have not yet measured directly. The reviewer-fatigue curve is a model, not an observation. Both are named in §5 as the first things to revisit.

What we gain. Ambiguous requests get a question instead of a guess. Runs survive restarts and multi-day pauses. Duplicate side effects are suppressed when the receiver atomically enforces stable idempotency keys and request fingerprints. And the control flow is explicit, which is what makes "why did the agent do that" answerable.

Alternatives considered

AlternativeWhy not
Keep the chainCannot express a backward edge. On the four requirement changes we scored it 12 damage points against the graph's 0 — every one of the four needs a rewrite, not an edit or a bolt-on.
Raw API calls, no frameworkGenuinely tempting, and correct if we needed nothing else — it's ~40 lines we'd own outright. But "pause for a human" and "survive a crash" are the same requirement, and hand-rolling durable resumable state is the single biggest chunk of work on the list.
CrewAI / multi-agentControl flow becomes emergent — who speaks next and when it stops are decided at runtime. Fine for exploration, hard to defend when someone asks why a decision was made. For insurance and fintech that rules it out.
12 smaller, cleaner nodes88.6% end-to-end instead of 94.1%, and 367 GB/month of checkpoints instead of 113 GB. Decomposition helps readability and costs reliability and storage — twice.
Redis for checkpointsSolves a write-rate problem we don't have (0.5% of OLTP) and re-creates the dual-write problem — now between an action and the record of it.
Review every refundTwo reviewers can process 6,400 items/month, leaving 113,600 over capacity. Auto-approval on timeout would be an unsafe fail-open policy; fail-closed expiry avoids unreviewed execution but creates a large blocked queue. The 6,400 seen arrive at 40/hour where catch rate has fallen to 25%.

What would change our mind

  • Measured error rate materially above 4% → the $170 threshold drops and the review queue grows; re-solve for staffing before changing the number.
  • Clarification loop resolving under ~40% → the loop isn't earning its latency; hand off on first ambiguity instead.
  • Checkpoint writes exceeding ~10% of OLTP → separate instance, or Redis hot tier with Postgres durable.
  • No pause and no durability requirement → drop the framework. Raw is cheaper and we'd own all of it.
  • A second genuinely independent specialist (fraud, say) → revisit crew-style orchestration, but only where the audit requirement is absent.

4. Three Domains

Retail

6 nodes · 1 loop
$170 gate · 3-day retention

The case where a framework is nearly over-engineering. Sessions last minutes, a lost conversation is an annoyance.

Worth saying so in an interview: reaching for LangGraph here is defensible mainly because of the approval pause, not the loop.

Life insurance

14 nodes · evidence loop
capacity-bound gate · 7-yr retention

Every expensive box is ticked. Multi-week runs, mandatory review, an audit trail, and a duplicate side effect that is a bound policy.

The checkpoint history is the audit trail — which makes delta checkpointing a design decision, not an optimisation.

Fintech

9 nodes · evidence loop
$465 gate · 180-day retention

The clock is the constraint. A 39-day review queue against a deadline measured in days is an automatic loss, not a cost.

The dead-letter path is a compliance control here: a run that fails and needs manual restart may miss the window.

The honest summary of this week
Three of the six ADR decisions depend on one number nobody has measured: the agent's actual error rate. The approval threshold, the retry economics, and the value of the review queue all move with it.

The models are sound and their sensitivity is explicit, which is the useful state to be in — but "we assumed 4%" is the sentence to volunteer rather than have extracted. Sampling 200 actions and grading them is a week of work and it firms up half this document.

7. Interview Drills

"Walk me through how you'd design an agent for customer refunds."

"I'd start by asking what it has to do when it's unsure, because that decides the shape. If there's no answer to that, a chain is fine and simpler. If it needs to ask a clarifying question and continue, that's a backward edge and you have a state machine whether or not you use a framework.

Then I'd count the nodes and keep it small. Reliability multiplies — six nodes at 99% each is 94.1% end to end, twelve is 88.6% — and full-checkpoint storage is quadratic in step count, so splitting a node costs you twice. That runs against the instinct to decompose, and it's worth being explicit about the tradeoff.

The approval gate I'd derive rather than pick. Review cost over error rate gives a first threshold, but that formula assumes reviewers never get tired. Once you model catch rate falling with queue depth, the optimum moves — on our numbers from $56 to $170 — and reviewing everything turns out to cost more and control less, because at 40 items an hour nobody's reading them.

Then idempotency keys before retries, backoff with jitter, and Postgres checkpointing on the instance we already have. Co-location can support a single transaction for local writes if we integrate both through it; external payment calls still require idempotency and an outbox or reconciliation path."

"Why not just use CrewAI? It's much less code."

"For exploration I might. The thing I'd want to be clear about is what you give up: control flow becomes emergent. Who speaks next, when it stops, and how disagreement resolves are decided by the framework and the models at runtime.

That's fine until someone asks why a particular decision was made, and the honest answer is 'the agents talked until they converged'. For insurance and fintech that's disqualifying on its own — you need the path to be a recorded fact, not an emergent property.

There's one row where crew is genuinely the natural answer, which is adding independent parallel specialists. If that's the requirement and there's no audit constraint, I'd use it and scope it tightly: pin who may speak, cap the turns, make the stopping rule explicit."

"Your agent double-refunded a customer. What happened and how do you prevent it?"

"Almost certainly an ambiguous failure: we called the payments API, the connection dropped before the response, and the retry issued a second refund. The request landed; we just never saw the acknowledgement.

The important framing is that this isn't a reliability problem — it's a correctness problem created by the reliability fix. Retries improve availability and damage correctness, and only idempotency lets you have both. So 'add retries' is never a complete answer; it's 'retries plus idempotency keys, and here's which operations need them.'

The fix is a UUID derived from the logical operation — this refund for this order — sent with the request and stored under a unique constraint at the receiver. The common way to get it wrong is generating the key per attempt, which is no key at all.

And the ordering matters: keys ship before retries. We modelled it for underwriting, where 0.156% of runs hit an ambiguous failure — four duplicate bound policies a month at $18,000 each, or $67,392, against a fix that's a header."

"How much does checkpointing cost, and why Postgres?"

"Less than people expect on write rate and more than they expect on storage, and both surprises are worth knowing.

On write rate: 6 steps at 1.3M runs a month is 7.8M writes, about 12 a second at peak. Against 2,400/sec of existing OLTP that's 0.5% — noise. People reach for Redis to solve a problem they haven't measured.

On storage: the usual estimate is runs times steps times state size, and that's wrong because state grows as the run proceeds. Writing the full state each step gives you 1+2+3+…+N, so it's quadratic. At six steps that's 3.6× the naive number; at twenty it's 11×. Delta checkpoints bring it back to linear.

Postgres because it keeps durable state near business data and can support one transaction for local writes when deliberately integrated. It is not automatically atomic with a framework checkpoint or an external action; those still need idempotency plus outbox/inbox or reconciliation. Co-location also simplifies backup and restore, provided recovery procedures keep checkpoint and business timelines consistent.

I'd move off Postgres if checkpoint writes became a large share of a shared instance's load, or if state were large and hot and read every step. And if runs are short enough that nothing needs to survive, I wouldn't checkpoint at all — an in-memory saver is a legitimate choice that nobody suggests."
