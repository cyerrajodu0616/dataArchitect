Hybrid Search in Production — Building It Inside Postgres

Week 2, Day 4 — query routing, fusion, and the evaluation trap that hides regressions

Week 1 Day 4 covered hybrid search as theory: the BM25 formula, why RRF is scale-invariant, k=60. Today is the production build — how you actually implement it in Postgres without a second search engine, how you decide per query how much lexical weight to apply, and the evaluation mistake that makes a hybrid rollout look successful while it quietly breaks your best-converting traffic.

Day 2 callback
Hybrid is the one Day-2 capability where pgvector's "you build it yourself" is genuinely fine — and where Postgres has a structural advantage the managed vector DBs lack. Your lexical index can be a generated column, which means the keyword index and the vector are updated by the same transaction that updates the product. Weaviate's built-in hybrid still lives on the far side of a sync pipeline.

1. Two Gaps, Not One

Most explanations only cover half of this. There are two failure directions, and hybrid exists because each retriever covers the other's blind spot.

That last point is the one worth memorising. People say "dense search is bad at exact match," which sounds like a recall problem. It is worse than that — it is a confident precision failure. The model returns a neighbouring SKU with a high similarity score, so nothing in your monitoring looks wrong. A zero-result page is a visible failure; a plausible wrong product at rank 1 is an invisible one.

2. The Retail Query Taxonomy

Blanket hybrid with a fixed weight is leaving money on the table, because retail query traffic is not homogeneous. Five classes, each wanting a different treatment:

"SKU-88213" · "MFG#4471-B" · "0714532198"
Exact identifier (~5–10% of traffic, very high intent and conversion). Wants exact lookup, not ranking. Dense contribution should be near zero.
"Salomon Quest 4 GTX" · "Dyson V15"
Brand + model (~20%). Rare, high-signal tokens. Lexical-dominant, but dense helps with variants and misspellings.
"waterproof hiking boots size 11 wide"
Attribute-constrained (~30%). Needs structured filters extracted from the query, plus balanced retrieval on the remainder.
"something warm for a rainy trek in Scotland"
Natural language / intent (~25%). Dense-dominant. BM25 contributes little and can actively hurt by matching "Scotland" in unrelated descriptions.
"watrproof hikking bots"
Misspelled (~10%). Dense is robust here (subword tokenisation); BM25 fails outright unless you add trigram fuzzy matching.

The taxonomy transfers — the identifiers and the stakes don't

Retail identifiers

SKU-88213 · MFG#4471-B · UPC 0714532198

~5–10% of traffic, ~4× conversion.

Getting it wrong: lost sale.

Insurance identifiers

ICC21-TL-NY · Form 10-R · policy A0473321 · ICD-10 E11.9

Form numbers are the dominant lookup for underwriters and agents. Note the extra class retail has none of: medical codes, where ICD-10 E11.9 and E11.29 are different conditions with different underwriting outcomes.

Getting it wrong: a compliance finding.

Fintech identifiers

merchant strings · account · txn ID · MCC 5812

Merchant normalisation is the canonical hybrid problem: AMZN MKTP US*2H4TR must resolve to Amazon, and lexical alone cannot while dense alone confuses similar merchants.

Getting it wrong: a mis-categorised transaction, or a missed fraud match.

The medical-code case is the sharpest argument for lexical retrieval you will find
Dense embeddings place E11.9 (type 2 diabetes without complications) and E11.29 (type 2 diabetes with kidney complications) almost on top of each other — the strings are nearly identical and so are the descriptions. Those two codes can mean accept at standard rates versus decline.

This is the lexical gap at its most consequential: the failure is confident, plausible, and silently wrong. In insurance, medical and form codes must go through exact lookup — never fusion, never nearest-neighbour. If you take one thing from Day 4 into an insurtech interview, take this example.

Why this taxonomy is the whole lesson
A single global alpha optimises the average query. But the exact-identifier class is 5–10% of traffic and often 3–5× the conversion rate of the average query — it is the customer who already knows exactly what they want. Tuning for the mean systematically underserves your most valuable segment. This is the argument for a query router, and it is the kind of point that separates a search architect from someone who wired up a vector index.

3. Building the Lexical Side Inside Postgres

You do not need Elasticsearch. You need a tsvector column, a GIN index, and awareness of one significant limitation.

-- The generated column is the key move: Postgres maintains the lexical index -- in the SAME transaction as the product update. No sync pipeline, no drift. ALTER TABLE products ADD COLUMN tsv tsvector GENERATED ALWAYS AS ( setweight(to_tsvector('english', coalesce(name, '')), 'A') || setweight(to_tsvector('english', coalesce(brand, '')), 'B') || setweight(to_tsvector('english', coalesce(description, '')), 'C') ) STORED; CREATE INDEX products_tsv_gin ON products USING GIN (tsv); -- Identifiers get their OWN path — never rely on FTS for exact lookup. -- 'english' config lowercases and splits SKU-88213 into 'sku' + '88213'. ALTER TABLE products ADD COLUMN sku_norm TEXT GENERATED ALWAYS AS (upper(regexp_replace(sku, '[^A-Za-z0-9]', '', 'g'))) STORED; CREATE INDEX products_sku_norm ON products (sku_norm); -- Trigram index for fuzzy/misspelled identifier and brand matching. CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS pg_trgm; CREATE INDEX products_name_trgm ON products USING GIN (name gin_trgm_ops);

Two things to notice, both worth saying out loud in an interview:

  • to_tsvector('english', ...) — the two-argument form with an explicit config is IMMUTABLE, which is what makes it legal in a generated column. The one-argument form depends on default_text_search_config, is only STABLE, and Postgres will reject it. This trips up almost everyone the first time.
  • setweight gives you field-level importance (name > brand > description) — the poor man's BM25F.
The limitation that matters: Postgres FTS ranking is not BM25
ts_rank_cd scores on term frequency and cover density (how tightly the query terms cluster in the document). It has no IDF term — no corpus-wide statistics at all. That means a rare, highly discriminative token is weighted the same as a common one.

For retail this is exactly backwards: the whole value of the lexical retriever is that rare tokens like a model number are decisive. Options, in order of cost: (a) accept it — RRF only uses rank, so the absolute score mattering less softens the blow; (b) hand-weight rare terms using your own document-frequency table; (c) install ParadeDB pg_search, which embeds a Tantivy index in Postgres and gives you real BM25 with IDF.

4. The Hybrid Query

Two CTEs, each producing a ranked list, fused by RRF. Note that k and the per-retriever weights are parameters — that is where the router plugs in.

-- Hybrid retrieval with weighted Reciprocal Rank Fusion. -- $1 = query text, $2 = query embedding, $3 = w_lexical, $4 = w_dense WITH lexical AS ( SELECT id, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY ts_rank_cd(tsv, websearch_to_tsquery('english', $1)) DESC) AS rank FROM products WHERE tsv @@ websearch_to_tsquery('english', $1) LIMIT 100 ), dense AS ( SELECT id, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY embedding <=> $2) AS rank FROM products ORDER BY embedding <=> $2 LIMIT 100 ) SELECT p.id, p.sku, p.name, COALESCE($3 / (60 + l.rank), 0) + COALESCE($4 / (60 + d.rank), 0) AS rrf_score FROM products p LEFT JOIN lexical l ON l.id = p.id LEFT JOIN dense d ON d.id = p.id WHERE l.id IS NOT NULL OR d.id IS NOT NULL ORDER BY rrf_score DESC LIMIT 10;
Why over-fetch 100 per retriever when you only return 10?

RRF can only fuse what each retriever surfaced. If lexical returns 10 and dense returns 10, a document that is rank 12 in both — a strong consensus candidate — never enters the fusion at all.

The rule: retrieve ~10× your final k from each arm. The cost is small (both indexes are already warm; you are paying for a longer result list, not another traversal) and the recall gain at the fusion stage is significant. If you later add a cross-encoder reranker, over-fetch further still — the reranker is the component that turns a wide, noisy candidate set into precision.

5. Fusion: RRF vs Weighted Score Normalisation

RRF (rank-based)Normalised score blend
InputRank position onlyActual scores, min-max or z-normalised per arm
Scale-invariant?Yes — the Week 1 Day 4 argumentNo — needs normalisation, which is distribution-sensitive
Preserves confidence?No — rank 1 by a landslide and rank 1 by a hair are identicalYes — a dominant match stays dominant
StabilityVery stable; k=60 works almost everywhereSensitive to outliers and to per-query score ranges
Best forDefault. Heterogeneous retrievers, no tuning budgetWhen one retriever is confidently right — e.g. exact identifier hits

The practical resolution: use RRF as the default, but bypass fusion entirely for the exact-identifier class. If sku_norm matches exactly, that is not a ranking signal to be blended — it is the answer. Blending it with dense results can only make it worse.

6. The Evaluation Trap

This is the section to remember. You roll out hybrid, you measure nDCG@10 over a held-out query set, and it improves from 0.61 to 0.66. You ship it. Three weeks later, revenue from search-originated sessions is flat or down.

What happened: aggregate metrics average over query classes with wildly different sizes and values.

Query class% of trafficnDCG@10 beforeafterΔConversion index
Natural language25%0.580.71+0.131.0×
Attribute-constrained30%0.620.70+0.081.4×
Misspelled10%0.440.63+0.190.8×
Brand + model27%0.730.70−0.032.1×
Exact identifier8%0.940.71−0.234.3×
Weighted aggregate0.6470.696+0.049—

The aggregate went up. The exact-identifier class — 8% of queries, converting at 4.3× the baseline — collapsed, because blending dense results into a query that had a perfect exact match diluted rank 1. The revenue-weighted outcome is negative while the headline metric is positive.

The rule
Never evaluate retrieval on an aggregate alone. Segment by query class, and weight by business value per class, not by traffic share. A change that improves the mean while regressing your highest-intent segment is a bad change wearing a good metric. The companion script reproduces this exact scenario numerically.

7. Operating Two Indexes

ConcernHow it behaves in Postgres
Keeping lexical in sync with the rowFree — generated column, same transaction. This is the Day 2 dual-write advantage applied to search.
Index sizeGIN on tsvector is small relative to HNSW — typically single-digit % of the vector index. Not a capacity concern.
GIN write amplificationReal. GIN uses a pending list (fastupdate); bulk loads can stall on cleanup. Tune gin_pending_list_limit or disable fastupdate for write-heavy tables.
Query planningTwo CTEs with LIMITs are planner-friendly, but verify with EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) that both arms use their index rather than a seq scan.
Failure isolationWrap each arm defensively: if the dense arm errors or times out, return lexical results rather than the whole query failing. Degraded search beats no search.
