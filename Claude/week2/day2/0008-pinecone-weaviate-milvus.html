Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus — What They Offer That pgvector Doesn't

Week 2, Day 2 — managed scale, native hybrid search, and multi-tenancy as first-class primitives

Day 1 covered how pgvector stores and indexes vectors inside Postgres. Today is the comparison you will actually be asked in an interview: "Why not a real vector database?" The wrong answer is a feature list. The right answer starts by reframing what class of problem a vector database solves — and that reframe is where your 14 years of data engineering is worth more than a GenAI engineer's vendor familiarity.

Retail framing
Scenario carried forward from Day 1
4M SKU embeddings, 500 QPS peak, sub-100ms P99. Now add three real requirements that Day 1 ignored:
  • Freshness: price and stock change every few minutes; a search result showing an out-of-stock item is a support ticket.
  • Hybrid: customers paste exact SKUs and part numbers as often as they type natural language.
  • Multi-tenancy: the platform serves 400 white-label storefronts. Storefront A must never retrieve storefront B's private catalog.

1. Reframe the Question First

Most comparisons ask "which database has better recall/latency?" That is the least interesting axis — all four of these systems run HNSW variants and land within a few milliseconds of each other at retail scale. The architecturally decisive question is:

Where does vector state live relative to your source of truth?

There are exactly two answers, and everything else follows from which one you pick:

  • Co-located (pgvector): the embedding is a column on the row it describes. Writing the SKU and writing its vector is one transaction.
  • Disaggregated (Pinecone / Weaviate / Milvus / Qdrant): the embedding lives in a second system. Writing the SKU and writing its vector are two writes to two systems.

You have spent 14 years dealing with the consequences of that second sentence. It has a name.

2. The Dual-Write Problem — pgvector's Structural Advantage

The moment vectors live outside your operational database, you own a distributed-systems consistency problem that no amount of vendor SLA removes. This is the single strongest argument in your favour, and it is one most GenAI engineers cannot articulate.

Two failure modes fall out of the right-hand diagram, and both have direct retail consequences:

  • Stale vector: the description changed, the embedding didn't. Search retrieves the product under its old meaning. A re-listed "winter parka" still matching queries for the discontinued "rain shell" it used to be.
  • Orphaned vector: the row was deleted, the vector delete failed. Search returns an ID that no longer joins to anything — a 404 in the result list, or worse, a delisted product still surfacing.

The standard fix is the transactional outbox pattern: write the row and an outbox event in one Postgres transaction, then a CDC consumer (Debezium/Kafka) drains the outbox into the vector DB with at-least-once delivery and idempotent upserts. You have almost certainly built this before for a warehouse sync. That is exactly the point — it is a real, known, staffed engineering cost, and it is line-item zero on every vendor's pricing page.

Interview trap
"Pinecone is fully managed, so operations are free." Managed means they operate the index. You still operate the sync pipeline, the reconciliation job, the drift dashboard, and the runbook for "vector store and Postgres disagree." The managed boundary sits at their API, not at your correctness requirement.

What drift costs, by domain

The dual-write problem is universal. What differs is what a stale vector actually does — and in two of these three domains it is not a quality problem.

Retail

Stale embedding on a re-listed product; orphaned vector for a delisted SKU.

Cost: a wrong or dead result in a search page. Measurable in conversion.

Life insurance

A rider is amended and refiled; the embedding still reflects the superseded wording.

Cost: an underwriter is shown guidance that is no longer in force. That is a mispriced risk and an audit finding — and you cannot prove which version was retrieved unless you designed for it.

Fintech

A merchant is reclassified or an account's risk tier changes; the vector store hasn't caught up.

Cost: fraud scoring on stale features. Under SR 11-7 model risk management you owe an explanation of the inputs — "the sync was 40 seconds behind" is one you have to be able to give.

The argument that ends the discussion in a regulated domain

There is a consideration in insurance and fintech that does not exist in retail, and it is usually decisive before any of the technical comparison matters.

An embedding derived from PHI or PII is, for regulatory purposes, still that data.

An embedding of an applicant's medical questionnaire is not anonymised. Embedding inversion research has repeatedly shown that substantial source text can be reconstructed from dense vectors, so the sensible compliance posture — and increasingly the one auditors take — is to treat the vector with the same controls as the record it came from. That has consequences:

  • The vector store enters your compliance scope. A third-party managed service now needs vendor security review, a data-processing agreement, and for PHI a signed BAA. Not every vector database vendor will sign one.
  • Data residency applies to the vectors. "Our Postgres is in-region but our vector index is wherever the vendor's serverless tier put it" is a finding.
  • Right-to-erasure gets harder. A deletion request must remove the row and the vector, and you must be able to evidence both. Across a dual-write boundary that is a reconciliation problem with a legal deadline attached.
  • Access control fragments. Postgres row-level security does not extend into an external index. You are reimplementing authorisation in a second system — and a retrieval bug becomes an unauthorised disclosure rather than a bad search result.
The one-liner for an insurtech or fintech interview
"Co-locating vectors in Postgres keeps sensitive data inside a boundary that's already been through security review, and keeps row-level security and deletion as single-system operations. Moving embeddings to a managed vector DB expands the compliance perimeter to a new vendor and turns right-to-erasure into a reconciliation problem with a regulatory deadline. In a regulated domain that usually settles it before we get to recall benchmarks."
Hold this one honestly too
It is an argument for data locality, not specifically for Postgres. A self-hosted Weaviate or Milvus inside your own VPC keeps the data in your perimeter as well. What pgvector uniquely preserves is that deletion, access control, and audit stay one system and one transaction. If someone pushes back with "we'd self-host Milvus in-VPC," the correct response is that they've solved residency but not the dual-write, RLS, or evidencing problems — not that they're wrong about residency.

3. Pinecone — Managed Serverless, Namespaces, Zero Knobs

Pinecone is the "you will never see an index parameter" option. It is closed-source and cloud-only — there is no self-hosted build, which is itself an architectural decision (a hard dependency on a single vendor's availability and pricing).

Architecture

Pinecone's serverless tier separates storage from compute: vectors live in cloud object storage, and query executors are spun up against the relevant slices on demand. You do not size nodes, choose an index type, or tune ef_search. You get an endpoint.

from pinecone import Pinecone pc = Pinecone(api_key="…") index = pc.Index("retail-catalog") # Namespaces are the multi-tenancy primitive — a logical partition inside one index. index.upsert( vectors=[{"id": "SKU-88213", "values": [...], "metadata": {"category": "boots", "price": 129.0}}], namespace="storefront-a", # <-- tenant isolation ) index.query( vector=[...], top_k=10, namespace="storefront-a", # query CANNOT cross namespaces filter={"category": {"$eq": "boots"}, "price": {"$lte": 150}}, include_metadata=True, )

What it genuinely buys you

  • Namespaces at scale. One index, thousands of isolated partitions, no per-tenant infrastructure. A namespace with zero queries costs only storage. This is the cleanest answer to the 400-storefront requirement.
  • No index tuning. There is no IVFFlat-vs-HNSW decision, no lists, no rebuild-after-bulk-load. Day 1's entire cold-start problem disappears.
  • Elastic cost at low volume. Serverless bills on storage + read units + write units, so an idle namespace is near-free. A 24/7 provisioned Postgres instance is not.
  • Integrated reranking and hosted embedding models, so a small team can ship retrieval without standing up model-serving infrastructure.
What you give up
  • Eventual consistency on writes. An upsert is not immediately queryable — there is a freshness lag measured in seconds. For a price-and-stock-sensitive catalog this must be designed around, not assumed away.
  • No joins. Metadata filters only. To render a result you fetch IDs from Pinecone and then hydrate from Postgres anyway — so you are running two systems on the read path too.
  • Metadata is duplicated. Every filterable attribute (category, price, stock) must be copied into Pinecone and kept in sync — which multiplies the dual-write surface from one field to many.
  • No exit. Closed source. Your migration path is a full re-index into another system.

4. Weaviate — Native Hybrid Search and Real Multi-Tenancy

Weaviate is open source (Go, BSD-3) with a managed cloud option. Of the three, it has the strongest answer to two of our three requirements: hybrid search and multi-tenancy.

Hybrid search is a first-class query, not an application-layer bolt-on

Recall Week 1 Day 4: BM25 handles exact SKU strings that dense vectors miss entirely (zero token overlap → dense embeddings have no anchor), and you fuse the two ranked lists with RRF. In Weaviate that is one parameter.

// alpha = 0.0 → pure BM25 keyword // alpha = 1.0 → pure vector // alpha = 0.5 → balanced fusion { Get { Product( hybrid: { query: "waterproof hiking boot SKU-88213" alpha: 0.6 fusionType: relativeScoreFusion # or rankedFusion (RRF, k=60) } limit: 10 ) { sku description _additional { score explainScore } } } }

Two fusion strategies matter architecturally:

  • rankedFusion — RRF. Uses only rank position, so it is scale-invariant (the Day 4 argument: BM25 scores are unbounded, cosine is bounded to [−1, 1], so you cannot add them directly).
  • relativeScoreFusion — normalises both score distributions and blends the actual values. Preserves the magnitude of a strong match, which rank-only fusion throws away. Generally better when one retriever is confidently right.

In pgvector you would implement this yourself: a tsvector column with ts_rank_cd (or ParadeDB's pg_search for real BM25), two CTEs, and a hand-written RRF join. Perfectly doable — you wrote that exact script on Day 4 — but it is your code to own and tune.

Multi-tenancy: a shard and an index per tenant

Weaviate's multi-tenancy is not a filter. Each tenant gets its own physical shard with its own HNSW graph, and tenants have a lifecycle: ACTIVE (in memory, queryable), INACTIVE (on local disk, not loaded), OFFLOADED (pushed to object storage).

from weaviate.classes.config import Configure, MultiTenancyConfig client.collections.create( name="Product", multi_tenancy_config=Configure.multi_tenancy( enabled=True, auto_tenant_creation=True, # tenant appears on first write auto_tenant_activation=True, # cold tenant warms on first query ), ) products = client.collections.get("Product").with_tenant("storefront-a") products.query.hybrid(query="waterproof hiking boot", alpha=0.6, limit=10)

For 400 storefronts where most are low-traffic, this is the operationally correct shape: a dormant storefront costs object-storage bytes, not RAM in a shared HNSW graph. In pgvector the equivalent is table-per-tenant (400 tables, 400 HNSW indexes, and Postgres catalog bloat) or a shared table with a tenant_id filter — which lands you straight back in Day 1's pre-filter problem.

Weaviate's other differentiator: vectorizer modules

Weaviate can call the embedding model for you at ingest and query time (text2vec-openai, text2vec-cohere, local transformers), and generative modules can run the RAG completion inside the query.

Architect's read: this is a genuine accelerator for a small team and a genuine anti-pattern for yours. It moves embedding generation — a batch, backfillable, cost-controlled, version-pinned pipeline concern — inside a database request path you don't control. When the model version changes you want a Spark job re-embedding 4M SKUs on your schedule with your retry semantics, not an implicit per-insert API call to OpenAI with the vendor's timeout policy. Say this out loud in an interview; it is exactly the judgment a platform architect is hired for.

5. Milvus — Distributed by Construction

Milvus (Apache 2.0, LF AI & Data; managed as Zilliz Cloud) is the one built for the billion-vector case. Its architecture will look immediately familiar to you — it is a disaggregated, log-structured, storage-compute-separated system in the same lineage as the warehouse engines you already operate.

Three things to take from that diagram, all of which map onto things you already know:

  1. Index build is isolated. Dedicated index nodes mean rebuilding a 4M-vector HNSW graph does not steal CPU from live queries. In pgvector, CREATE INDEX and your Black Friday traffic share one machine — the reason Day 1's IVFFlat rebuild cadence is a real operational constraint.
  2. Log-as-data. Writes land in a message queue first; workers consume and materialise segments. This is the same design as a streaming lakehouse, and it is why Milvus can absorb high-velocity ingestion without blocking reads.
  3. Tunable consistency. Milvus exposes consistency as a per-query knob — Strong, BoundedStaleness, Session, Eventual. Pinecone gives you eventual and no dial.
from pymilvus import MilvusClient client = MilvusClient(uri="http://milvus:19530") # Consistency is a query-time decision, not a deployment-time one. client.search( collection_name="products", data=[query_vec], limit=10, filter='category == "boots" and price <= 150', consistency_level="Bounded", # Strong | Bounded | Session | Eventual partition_names=["storefront_a"], )
Milvus's index zoo — and why breadth of index support actually matters

pgvector gives you two ANN indexes (IVFFlat, HNSW). Milvus gives you roughly a dozen, and the ones that matter architecturally are the memory-reduction families:

  • IVF_SQ8 / IVF_PQ — scalar and product quantization. PQ can cut memory ~10–20× at a recall cost, which is how you fit a billion vectors in affordable RAM.
  • DiskANN — graph index designed to live on NVMe rather than RAM. This is the "your index no longer has to fit in memory" escape hatch, and it is the single biggest cost lever at 100M+ scale.
  • GPU_CAGRA / GPU_IVF_* — GPU-resident indexes for very high QPS.

pgvector has partially closed this gap: halfvec (fp16, halves storage), binary quantization, and sparsevec arrived in 0.7. And the Timescale extension pgvectorscale brings StreamingDiskANN plus statistical binary quantization to Postgres directly. Know that this gap is narrowing — asserting "pgvector has no quantization" in an interview dates you.

6. Sidebar: Qdrant and the Filtered-ANN Problem

Qdrant (Rust, Apache 2.0) is not on the plan's list but belongs in your answer, because it directly solves the limitation Day 1 ended on.

Day 1's pre-filter dilemma: with WHERE category = 'boots', you either run ANN then filter (fast, but may return fewer than k results) or filter then exact-scan (accurate, but loses ANN's speed). Qdrant's filterable HNSW pushes the payload filter into the graph traversal — the search only walks nodes that satisfy the predicate, and the graph is built with extra links so the filtered subgraph stays connected. Weaviate and Milvus have their own filtered-search implementations in the same spirit.

Update to your Day 1 answer
pgvector 0.8 added iterative index scans, which re-probe the index when a filter eliminates too many candidates instead of returning a short result set. That closes the worst of the pre-filter gap without leaving Postgres. Filtered ANN is no longer a reason on its own to adopt a dedicated vector DB — but highly selective filters at high QPS still favour a filter-aware graph.

8. Multi-Tenancy — The Requirement That Actually Forces the Decision

Of our three added requirements, freshness favours pgvector and hybrid is a wash (you can build it). Multi-tenancy at 400 storefronts is the one where the purpose-built systems have a structural answer and Postgres has a workaround.

Retail — 400 storefronts

White-label catalogs. Traffic heavily skewed: a handful of large storefronts, a long tail of dormant ones.

Isolation need: commercial. Storefront A must not see B's catalog or pricing.

Insurtech — N carriers / distributors

A platform underwriting on behalf of multiple carriers, each with its own filed forms and guidelines. This is the same shape as the retail case with the stakes raised.

Isolation need: contractual and regulatory. Carrier A's underwriting guidelines are trade secrets; leakage is a breach of contract, not a UX bug.

Fintech — BaaS program tenants

Sponsor-bank programs, each with its own disclosures and risk policies.

Isolation need: regulatory. Program separation is examined.

Model 1 is the one to argue against hardest, and the argument is a data-engineering one, not a security one: a shared ANN graph makes recall a function of tenant size. A storefront with 2,000 SKUs inside a 4M-vector graph is a highly selective filter — exactly the case where ANN traversal wanders through millions of irrelevant neighbours before finding enough matching candidates. Small tenants get measurably worse search than large ones, and the metric that exposes it (recall segmented by tenant size) is not on any default dashboard.

12. Postgres Memory Limits — What Actually Caps You

Day 3 shows that the HNSW index must stay resident or P99 collapses. The natural next question is the one to ask: how much memory can Postgres actually use? There are four distinct ceilings, and only one of them is the real one.

Ceiling 1 — Postgres itself: effectively none

shared_buffers is configured in 8 kB blocks as a signed integer, so the theoretical maximum is around 16 TB. No one has hit this. Postgres is not the constraint.

But raising shared_buffers is not how you win here, for a reason specific to Postgres's I/O model:

Postgres uses buffered I/O, not direct I/O — so pages live in shared_buffers and the OS page cache.

Set shared_buffers to 80% of RAM and you get double buffering: the same index pages cached twice, wasting the memory you were trying to use. The conventional 25% guidance exists for this reason. For pgvector the practical consequence is liberating:

What actually matters for vector workloads
Total machine RAM, not shared_buffers. A cached HNSW page is fast whether it is cached by Postgres or by the OS. So size the instance against index size (Day 3's model), leave shared_buffers near conventional values, and let the OS page cache do the rest. Tuning shared_buffers upward is the wrong lever — and knowing that is worth more than memorising a number.

Ceiling 2 — Per-connection memory, which multiplies

SettingScopeRisk
work_memPer sort/hash node, per connectionThe classic outage: a complex plan uses it several times over, × hundreds of connections. Budget conservatively and raise per-session.
maintenance_work_memPer index build / VACUUMToo small and an HNSW build spills to disk and takes roughly 5× longer. Set it high per session for builds, never globally.
Backend overheadPer connection (a real OS process)Why the Day 3 RAM cliff compounds — 40× latency means 40× concurrent backends. PgBouncer.

Ceiling 3 — The instance you can actually buy

This is the ceiling that binds in practice, and it is economic rather than technical. Managed Postgres memory-optimised instances top out in the region of 1–4 TB of RAM (exact ceilings differ by provider, engine and region — check current instance classes rather than trusting a remembered number). Self-managed bare metal goes further, into the tens of TB, at prices that stop being reasonable well before the hardware stops existing.

Against Day 3's model — ~7 GB of index per million vectors at 1536 dims — a 1 TB instance holds roughly 80M vectors before the ladder, and several hundred million after halfvec + MRL truncation. That is the real single-node frontier.

Ceiling 4 — The one that surprises people: pgvector's index dimension limit

This one bites in practice, and it is not a memory limit at all
An index entry must fit inside a single 8 kB Postgres page. That imposes hard dimension caps on indexes that are much lower than the caps on the types:
TypeMax dims (column)Max dims (HNSW / IVFFlat index)
vector (float32)16,0002,000
halfvec (fp16)16,0004,000
bit (binary)—64,000
Notice the arithmetic: 2,000 × 4 bytes = 8,000 B, and 4,000 × 2 bytes = 8,000 B. Both caps are the same 8 kB page, seen twice.

The practical consequence: text-embedding-3-large is 3,072 dimensions. You cannot build an HNSW index on a vector(3072) column — the column works, inserts work, and then CREATE INDEX fails. Your options are halfvec(3072) (3,072 ≤ 4,000, so indexable, and you get Day 3's 1.9× memory win for free) or MRL-truncate to 1,536 or 2,000 dimensions.

Why this is a good thing to know cold
It is a concrete, checkable limit that catches teams after they have embedded a corpus with the larger model. It also reframes halfvec: Day 3 sold it as a memory optimisation, but at 3,072 dimensions it is not an optimisation at all — it is the only way to have an index. Two independent reasons for the same decision is a strong position to argue from.

13. Distributed Vector Search — How Sharding Actually Works

Day 3's conclusion was that replication scales QPS but not corpus size, because every replica holds the whole index — so sharding is the only structural answer to volume. That raises the question this section answers: how do you shard an ANN index at all?

It is harder than sharding relational data, for one reason:

Nearest-neighbour search has no partition key. The answer can be on any shard.

A relational query with WHERE customer_id = 42 routes to one shard. "Find the 10 most similar vectors" has no such handle — similarity is a property of the whole space. There are exactly three strategies, and each trades something different away.

Strategy A — Random shard + scatter-gather (the common one)

Distribute vectors arbitrarily. Every query goes to every shard; each returns its local top-k; a coordinator merges. Used by Milvus, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Vespa.

  • Recall: preserved. The true top-k is a subset of the union of per-shard top-k.
  • Volume: scales linearly. This is the point.
  • QPS: does not scale with shards — every query costs one search on every shard. Adding shards raises capacity but not per-query throughput.
  • Latency: you wait for the slowest shard on every single query.

That last point deserves a diagram, because it is the failure mode people are least prepared for:

Tail amplification — the counter-intuitive part
Each query samples every shard's latency distribution and takes the maximum. With 10 shards, a per-shard P99 of 10 ms means roughly a 1 − 0.9910 ≈ 10% chance that at least one shard is in its tail — so your query-level P90 already looks like the shard-level P99. Scaling out to 100 shards makes it a near-certainty on every request.

This is Dean & Barroso's "The Tail at Scale," and it is why distributed vector search needs hedged requests, per-shard timeouts with partial results, and hard work on tail latency that a single node simply does not require. Sharding does not just add capacity — it adds a latency problem you did not previously have.

Strategy B — Semantic sharding (route by vector space region)

Partition the space itself — coarse centroids, one region per shard — and route each query only to the shards whose regions are near it. This is IVFFlat's idea promoted to a cluster topology (SPANN and similar).

  • QPS scales — each query touches only a few shards.
  • Recall suffers at boundaries — true neighbours just across a region edge are missed unless you over-probe.
  • Rebalancing is painful — as data drifts, regions become unbalanced and re-partitioning means moving vectors between nodes.

Strategy C — Shard on a business key (the one you should want)

If your queries always carry a partition key, you avoid the whole problem
When every query is scoped — retail storefront_id, insurance carrier_id, fintech tenant_id — you can shard on that key. Each query becomes single-shard: no scatter, no tail amplification, no recall loss, and QPS scales linearly with nodes. Each shard's index is small enough to stay resident, which also defuses Day 3's RAM cliff.

This is why Day 2's multi-tenancy section matters more than it first appears. A tenancy model is also a sharding strategy — and it is the only one that gives you distribution without paying for it in either recall or tail latency. It is available in plain Postgres via partitioning or Citus, and it is exactly the kind of thing 14 years of data modelling prepares you to spot.

The escalation ladder, stated as an interview answer
"Before I'd migrate for volume, I'd try declarative partitioning by tenant — that's still plain Postgres and it makes each query single-shard. If I outgrow one node, Citus shards the same pgvector tables across nodes and keeps my SQL and my transactional writes. Only when there's no usable partition key and the corpus genuinely exceeds what partitioned Postgres can hold would I move to Milvus — and at that point I'm accepting scatter-gather tail amplification and a permanent sync pipeline as the price of the volume."
