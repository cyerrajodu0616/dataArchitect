RAG Failure Modes — Irrelevant, Stale, and Contradictory

Week 3, Day 5 — the failure that passes every metric you built yesterday

New to this? Read Day 0 first

This lesson assumes the vocabulary from Day 0 — RAG From Zero: what RAG is, chunks, embeddings, top-k, recall. Twenty minutes there saves an hour here.

What today is about, in plain words

Somebody reports that the assistant gave a wrong answer. "Wrong" is not a diagnosis — there are about six completely different things that could have happened, and they have six different fixes. Picking the wrong one costs you a quarter.

Most of today is about one of the six, because it is the one that is genuinely hard to see. Here it is:

Your policy document was revised in 2021 and again in 2024. All three versions of the exclusion clause are sitting in your index — you kept them, correctly, because a 2019 claim has to be adjudicated against the 2019 wording. The three versions say almost the same thing in almost the same words.

A question comes in. Search finds all three, because near-identical text is near-identical in meaning-space. The model receives three slightly contradictory clauses, is told nothing about which one is in force, and picks one. Maybe the right one. The answer is fluent, correctly quoted, and properly cited — so every quality check you built yesterday says it's fine.

Anchoring this to what you already know — and you know this one cold

This is a slowly changing dimension problem. You have three versions of a row, no effective_from / effective_to on the join, and no is_current flag. The query returns all three and something downstream picks arbitrarily.

You would never ship that in a warehouse. You would put validity windows on the dimension and an as-of date on the query, and you'd have done it without being asked.

Where it breaks — the reason this is worth a whole day: in SQL, forgetting the date predicate returns three rows and somebody notices. Here it returns one confident paragraph. There is no duplicate-row symptom, no row count to reconcile. The bug is invisible at every layer of monitoring, and it surfaces months later in a dispute. This is a Type-2 SCD problem that has been made unobservable, and spotting that is the single most transferable thing you bring to a GenAI team.

irrelevant retrieval
Plain: we fetched the wrong chunks. The correct one exists but wasn't found or wasn't ranked high enough. Days 1–3.
stale retrieval
Plain: two different things wearing one name. (a) Index staleness — the document changed and the index hasn't caught up yet, a pipeline lag problem. (b) Corpus staleness — the index is perfectly up to date and contains an old version on purpose, because you're required to keep it. Only (b) is today's problem, and only (b) is unfixable by running the pipeline more often.
conflicting sources
Plain: the retrieved chunks disagree with each other, usually because they are different versions of the same clause.
grounded
Plain: every claim in the answer traces to a retrieved chunk. Note what it does not mean: correct, current, or applicable. An answer quoting a superseded clause word-perfect is fully grounded. That gap is the whole lesson.
as-of date
Plain: the date the question should be answered as of. For a 2023 claim that is 2023, not today. Your terms: the effective-date predicate on a bitemporal join — and hardcoding today() is the classic bug.
detection control vs prevention control
Plain: a prevention control makes the bad thing impossible — filter superseded versions out before search ever sees them. A detection control notices afterwards and tries to compensate — telling the model "prefer the newest version". Detection can only choose among what was retrieved, so if the current version wasn't retrieved at all it has nothing to work with. Audit committees ask which one you have.

0. Start Here — Watch It Happen on the Running Example

Same policy, three versions of one clause, all legitimately in the index:

One clause, three versions — all three must be retained
D2·v1This rider does not pay if death results from suicide within two years of issue.  — effective 2019-03-01 to 2021-06-30
D2·v2This rider does not pay if death results from suicide within two years of issue or reinstatement.  — effective 2021-07-01 to 2024-02-29
D2·v3This rider does not pay if death results from suicide within one year of issue or reinstatement.  — effective 2024-03-01 to open
Q: "is a suicide claim payable 18 months after the policy was issued?" (the policy in question was issued in 2020)

The correct answer is no — the 2020 policy is governed by v1, and 18 months is inside the two-year exclusion.

The counter-intuitive part: your Day 3 reranker caused this
Before the reranker, versions competed against the whole corpus and usually only one made the cut. A cross-encoder scores every candidate against the query independently — it has no view of the result set as a whole — and since all three versions genuinely answer the question, it promotes all three.

Measured in §5: retail goes from 0.1% to 97.5% of affected queries carrying multiple versions. Day 3's precision win and this failure are the same mechanism seen from two sides. Which produces a hard sequencing rule: the effective-date filter must ship before the reranker, not after — otherwise you manufacture a failure mode that did not previously exist.

Try this — the fix, and why the obvious version isn't one
The tempting fix is a line in the prompt: "if sources conflict, prefer the most recent." Trace it against the case above. The policy was issued in 2020, so the correct source is v1 — the oldest one. "Prefer the most recent" gives you v3 and confidently the wrong answer, faster.

The real fix is a WHERE clause you already know how to write: effective_from <= :as_of AND effective_to > :as_of, with :as_of being the policy issue date — not today(). Filter before search, and there is only one version to retrieve. Prevention, not detection.

Day 4 built the measurement apparatus. Today is what it catches, what it misses, and how to tell failures apart — because the symptom "the answer was wrong" has at least six distinct causes with six different fixes.

The bulk of the lesson is the one failure mode that passes every metric in Day 4: conflicting sources. It produces a fluent, correctly-cited answer drawn from a real retrieved document, and groundedness scores it as grounded — because it is.

2. Retrieval Failures

FailureWhat happensThe non-obvious part
The miss The answer never enters the candidate set. Bounded by recall@N, which is Day 3's ceiling. Nothing downstream can recover it — this is the only failure where adding a reranker is provably useless.
The distractor Irrelevant-but-similar chunks fill the context. Adding more context can make answers worse. More retrieved chunks is not monotonically better, and "we increased k and quality dropped" is a real and confusing result.
The near-miss A chunk that looks right and is subtly wrong — E11.9 instead of E11.29, reason code 4837 instead of 4853. Day 2 §3: this is dense retrieval's characteristic failure and it is silent. The lexical arm exists to catch it.
Truncation at ingest The answer was split across chunk boundaries and no chunk contains it whole. Day 1 §2: if chunk size < answer span, containment is zero. Looks exactly like a retrieval miss and no retrieval change fixes it.

3. Position Matters — Lost in the Middle

Models attend more reliably to the beginning and end of a long context than to its middle. This has a direct, cheap consequence for how you assemble the prompt:

Order the context deliberately, not by rank
The naive assembly — paste chunks in descending relevance — puts your second-best chunk in the middle, which is the weakest position. Interleave instead: best first, second-best last, and the rest in between. It costs nothing, requires no model change, and it is the single cheapest quality improvement in this lesson.

It also raises the stakes on k. A long context has more middle. Day 3's argument for a smaller, better-ranked candidate set and this are the same argument.

The second kind is not a bug in any component. The old version of a policy form is supposed to be in the corpus — you cannot delete it, because a claim filed in 2023 must be adjudicated under the 2023 wording. The corpus is correct. Retrieval is correct. The answer is wrong.

5. Conflicting Sources — The Failure That Passes Every Metric

A revised document produces several near-identical chunks: v1, v2, v3 of the same clause, differing by one sentence. And that one sentence is the entire legal content.

Because they are near-identical, they sit almost on top of each other in embedding space. So a retrieval that finds one tends to find them all — and the model receives three versions of the same rule, is told nothing about which is in force, and picks one.

How often does this happen?

python conflict_and_staleness.py --domain insurance strategy conflict wrong only slots lost -------------------------------------------------------------------------- nothing (the default) 36.0% 28.9% 0.46 cross-encoder rerank, version-blind 100.0% 0.3% 2.95 version-aware dedup (keep newest) 0.0% 0.0% 0.00 effective-date WHERE clause 0.0% 0.0% 0.00
The result worth staring at: reranking causes this
Insurance goes from 36% to 100%. Retail goes from 0.1% to 97.5%. A cross-encoder scores each candidate against the query, independently — and every version answers the query about equally well, so a reranker that is working correctly promotes all of them.

Day 3's precision win and this failure are the same mechanism seen from two sides. Retail is the alarming case: the problem is essentially absent at baseline and near-universal once you add the reranker you just spent Day 3 justifying. If you ship a reranker without version handling, you have manufactured a failure mode that did not previously exist.

What actually fixes it

ControlTypeEffectNote
Effective-date WHERE clause Prevention Conflicts → 0% One indexed predicate. Superseded versions never become candidates, so nothing downstream can promote them.
Version-aware dedup Prevention (post hoc) Conflicts → 0% Fallback when "current" isn't expressible as a date. Must keep the newest, not the highest-scoring — retrieval score says nothing about which version is in force.
"Cite the effective date and prefer the newest" Detection only Cannot fix 28.9% Belongs in the prompt anyway. But it can only choose among what was retrieved — and in 28.9% of insurance queries the context held a version without the current one. No instruction helps when the right text isn't there.
Detection vs prevention — the distinction an audit will ask about
The prompt-level fix is a detection control: it surfaces the ambiguity so a human sees there was a choice. The date filter is a prevention control: the wrong version never reaches the model. When a regulator asks "how do you ensure the current form was used?", "we ask the model to prefer the newest" is not an answer. "Superseded versions are excluded by an indexed predicate at query time" is.

The as-of date is the load-bearing detail

-- Week 2 Day 1 built this schema. Here is what it was for. ALTER TABLE chunk ADD COLUMN clause_key text; -- stable identity across versions ALTER TABLE chunk ADD COLUMN doc_version int; ALTER TABLE chunk ADD COLUMN effective_from date; ALTER TABLE chunk ADD COLUMN effective_to date; -- NULL = currently in force ALTER TABLE chunk ADD COLUMN jurisdiction text; CREATE INDEX ON chunk (clause_key, effective_from DESC); -- every retrieval carries an as-of date, and it is NOT today() WHERE effective_from <= :as_of AND (effective_to IS NULL OR effective_to > :as_of) AND jurisdiction IN (:state, 'ALL')
Always filtering to "current" is also wrong
For a claim filed in 2023, the question is what the policy said then. A system hardcoded to today() fails quietly and in the direction that loses litigation. The as-of date is an input to the query, not a constant, and defaulting it to today is a decision that should be made explicitly and written down.

Note also that this is the same predicate Week 2 §10 identified as the load-bearing architecture question. Filter selectivity decides whether you need an ANN index at all; here the same clause is a correctness control. It earns its keep twice.

6. Why Day 4's Metrics Cannot See This

Every other failure in this lesson announces itself. Retrieval misses produce visibly unhelpful answers. Hallucinations contradict the source. Stale indexes produce complaints. A version conflict produces a fluent, well-cited, plausible answer drawn from a real document that was true last year.

The eval you have to build on purpose
Questions whose answer changed between versions, scored against the version effective on a stated as-of date. Twenty of them is enough to know whether you have the problem.

If your golden set contains no such questions, you have no evidence at all about this failure mode — not weak evidence, none. And your relevance graders will have made it worse: shown three versions of a clause, a grader marks all three relevant, because they are. The labelling process itself erases the distinction.

7. Three Domains

Retail

2 versions · k=8
baseline 0.1% → reranked 97.5%

Policy pages get revised; product copy gets duplicated across variants.

The fix is cheaper here: retail can simply delete the old version — an option regulated domains do not have.

But note the reranker effect. Do not assume low stakes means no problem.

Life insurance

4 versions · k=6
baseline 36% · wrong-only 28.9%

Forms are amended and re-filed by state. Four versions of a suicide exclusion is normal.

A version conflict here is a regulatory finding, not a bad search result.

Old versions cannot be deleted — claims are adjudicated under the wording in force at the time.

Fintech

3 versions · k=6

Network rules are revised on a published schedule; old reason-code tables must stay.

Disputes are adjudicated under the rules in force at transaction time — so the as-of date comes from the transaction, not the request.

This is the clearest case that today() is the wrong default.
