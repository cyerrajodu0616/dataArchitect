What an ADR is, and why the week ends with one

An ADR — Architecture Decision Record — is a one-page document that says: here was the situation, here were the options, here's what we chose, here's why, and here's what we gave up. It's written once, dated, and never edited; if the decision changes later you write a new one that supersedes it.

The point is not documentation for its own sake. It's that in eighteen months someone — possibly you — will look at the pipeline and ask "why on earth is the overlap 60%?" Without an ADR the answer is a shrug, and the setting either stays forever out of superstition or gets changed by someone who doesn't know what it was protecting.

For interview purposes this is the deliverable that separates the levels. An engineer describes what they built. An architect produces a document where every number is traceable to a measurement and every decision names the evidence that would reverse it.

The week in six sentences, before the formal version

Day 0: RAG means finding the relevant passages and pasting them into the prompt. The model is the easy part.

Day 1: Cut documents into chunks. If an answer gets cut in half, that question is dead forever — and you buy the guarantee with overlap, which is paid once, not with chunk size, which is paid in every prompt.

Day 2: Search two ways. Word matching can't find "suicide" when the user typed "self-inflicted"; meaning matching returns the wrong form number with a confident score. Merge both lists with RRF.

Day 3: A second, slower model re-reads the top 100 alongside the question and re-sorts. It fixes ordering; it can never recover a chunk the first stage didn't return.

Day 4: Measure with a golden set — and check whether your golden set is even large enough to see the effect you're arguing about. Usually it isn't.

Day 5: The nastiest failure is retrieving three versions of the same clause and answering from whichever one turns up. Every metric says the answer is grounded, because it is.

Week 2 ended with ADR-002: why pgvector. That decision was about where vectors live. This one is about what happens between a user's question and the model's answer — five decisions, each with a number behind it rather than a preference.

What makes this an architect's document rather than a design doc
Every decision below states the quantity that drove it, what it costs, and what would change our mind. The last one is the part people skip, and it is the part that makes a decision reviewable a year later by someone who wasn't in the room.

1. The Pipeline

2. ADR-003

ACCEPTED   ADR-003: Retrieval pipeline architecture and evaluation framework

Context

ADR-002 established pgvector as the vector store. It did not specify how documents become chunks, how candidates are retrieved and ordered, or how any change to those is evaluated. In practice those decisions were made implicitly — 800-token chunks with 15% overlap because that is the framework default, dense-only retrieval, top-8 straight into the prompt, and quality judged by inspection.

Three problems forced this ADR. First, cost: at 20 QPS the generation bill is dominated by chunk size, and nobody had costed it. Second, quality: several classes of query fail in ways the team could describe but not measure. Third, and decisively — we could not tell whether any change we made was an improvement, because the 45-query evaluation set cannot resolve the size of effect we were arguing about.

Decision

  1. Chunking: 200-token chunks with 20% overlap, structure-aware where documents have headings, recursive splitting otherwise. Overlap is set from the measured p90 answer-span length, not from a default.
  2. Retrieval: hybrid BM25 + dense with RRF fusion, field-weighted tsvector (setweight A/B/D), and a per-arm score floor applied before fusion so "no good answer" is expressible.
  3. Correctness filter: every query carries an as-of date; superseded document versions are excluded by an indexed predicate. This ships before the reranker.
  4. Reranking: MiniLM cross-encoder over N=50 candidates, applied only to the NATURAL_LANGUAGE and AMBIGUOUS classes.
  5. Evaluation: golden set of 550 graded queries stratified by class, including ~30 unanswerable and ~20 version-change questions. Every change is judged by a paired bootstrap with 95% intervals, reported per class, in CI.

Consequences

Accepted costs. 20M chunks instead of 4.7M — index infrastructure rises from $1,155 to $4,910/month. A GPU fleet enters the architecture, which is a new operational surface (~$4,672/month routed). Building the golden set is roughly three weeks of labelling effort, and it is the largest single cost in this document.

Accepted risks. Chunk-size and overlap choices rest on a p90 answer-span measurement from 50 examples; if that estimate is materially wrong the whole configuration shifts. The reranker adds a failure domain that can degrade independently of Postgres.

What we gain. Total retail cost falls from $27,574 to $20,449/month despite adding a reranker, because the generation bill dominates and smaller chunks shrink it faster than the index grows. Version conflicts go to zero. And every subsequent change becomes measurable, which is the point.

Alternatives considered

AlternativeWhy not
Keep 800-token chunks$27,574/month vs $20,449 — 35% more, for worse signal density (5.0% vs 20.0%). The index-only cost model that justified large chunks was measuring the smaller half of the bill.
Semantic chunkingAdds an embedding pass per sentence at ingest plus a threshold to maintain, for gains that are modest against good recursive splitting. Our documents have headings; structure-aware chunking captures most of the benefit for none of the cost.
Dense-only retrievalFails on SKUs, model numbers and specs — an entire query class where the embedding has no useful opinion and returns confident neighbours. Day 2 §3.
Listwise LLM rerankerDoes not fit. At 512-token chunks even N=25 exceeds a 2-second budget, and N=10 costs 28× a cross-encoder for a worse ceiling. Its place is offline labelling.
N=200 (the largest that fits latency)$29,200/month vs $7,592 at N=50 — 3× the cost per unit of ceiling. "Fits the budget" is not a sizing criterion.
Keep the 45-query eval setIts minimum detectable effect is ~0.066 nDCG. The changes in this ADR are worth +0.02 to +0.05. It cannot see them, and would have reported a number anyway.

What would change our mind

  • Measured p90 answer span above ~400 tokens → 200-token chunks become untenable; move to small-to-big (Day 1 §9) rather than simply enlarging chunks.
  • RAG traffic falls below ~2 QPS → generation stops dominating, the U-curve flattens, and larger chunks become the cheaper choice again.
  • Measured recall@100 below 0.90 → deprioritise the reranker entirely and spend the quarter on retrieval. A reranker on a candidate set without the answer reorders wrong answers.
  • Per-class evaluation shows fused ≈ dense-only → the lexical arm is contributing nothing; diagnose it (IDF, text config, field weights) before accepting hybrid's cost.
  • Corpus grows past ~100M chunks → this is an ADR-002 question again, not an ADR-003 one. Week 2 §9 applies.

The line that reframes the conversation
Generation is 53% of the retail bill and the vector index is 24%. Week 2 spent five days on the index because that is where the interesting engineering is; this week found that the prompt is where the money is. Both facts are worth carrying — the index is where you can break the system, and the prompt is where you can bankrupt it.

5. Rollout Order

The order matters, and two of these dependencies are not obvious:

#ShipWhy here
1Golden set + paired bootstrap in CIEverything after this is unmeasurable without it. It's also the long pole — start the labelling now.
2Effective-date filterBefore the reranker, not after. Shipping the reranker first takes retail version-conflict from 0.1% to 97.5%.
3Context ordering (best first and last)Free, immediate, no dependencies. Do it while labelling.
4Field-weighted tsvector + score floorPure SQL. Fixes the largest share of the missing-IDF problem for the least work.
5Re-chunk to 200/20%Needs content_hash in place first, or every future chunking change is a full re-embed.
6Span dedup + mergeMust land with the overlap increase, not after. 20% overlap without dedup buys answer survival and pays for it in result diversity.
7Reranker, routedLast. It's the most expensive, and steps 2 and 5 change what it's worth.

6. Interview Drills

"Walk me through how you'd size the chunks for a RAG system."

"I'd start by measuring the answer-span length — take 50 real questions, find the passage that answers each, record its token length, and use the p90. That number sets two things: the chunk has to be bigger than it, and the overlap should be at least as large as it, because overlap at least the span length guarantees no answer is ever split across a boundary.

Then I'd pick the smallest chunk above that floor, because signal density falls as 1/C and you pay for the noise twice — once in index RAM and again in every prompt that retrieves it.

The part people miss is that chunk size moves two costs in opposite directions. Smaller chunks mean more vectors, so the index grows; but they also mean fewer tokens per retrieved chunk, so the prompt shrinks. On our retail numbers generation is about 80× the infrastructure at large chunk sizes, so total cost is U-shaped with a minimum around 200 tokens — and an index-only cost model would have pointed at 1,600, which is three times the optimum.

One caveat I'd state: that U-shape assumes a fixed k. If you hold the context budget constant and retrieve more smaller chunks, generation cost is flat and only the index moves, so larger chunks win. Which policy is right is an empirical question about recall at a fixed context budget, and I'd measure it rather than assume."

"Your reranker is excellent but quality hasn't moved. What do you do?"

"I'd compute recall@N of the first stage before touching the reranker, because it's a hard ceiling — the reranker only sees what it was handed. If recall@100 is 0.72, then 28% of queries are unanswerable no matter how good the reranker is, and I'd stop and fix retrieval: chunking, hybrid weights, query expansion.

The other thing I'd check is the gap between recall@k and recall@N. If the first stage already puts the answer in the top 8, there's no headroom and the reranker is pure latency — that's a different diagnosis with the same symptom.

And I'd want to know whether the eval set can even see the change. A 50-query golden set has a minimum detectable effect around 0.06 nDCG, and a realistic reranker gain is 0.02 to 0.05. 'Quality hasn't moved' might mean the reranker isn't working, or it might mean we can't measure it — and those need different responses."

"How do you stop a RAG system answering from an outdated policy?"

"An indexed effective-date predicate at query time, with the as-of date as an input rather than today() — for a claim filed in 2023 the question is what the policy said then.

What I'd want to convey is why the alternatives don't work. Instructing the model to prefer the newest version is a detection control, not a prevention control: it can only choose among what was retrieved, and we measured that in about 29% of queries the context held a superseded version without the current one. No instruction helps when the right text isn't there.

The thing I'd flag proactively is that this interacts badly with reranking. Versions of a clause are near-identical, so they're near-identical in embedding space, and a cross-encoder scores each candidate against the query independently — every version answers the query equally well, so it promotes all of them. We measured retail going from 0.1% version-conflict to 97.5% purely from adding a reranker. So the date filter has to ship before the reranker, not after.

And I'd note that our standard metrics can't catch this. Groundedness passes, because the claim genuinely is supported by a retrieved chunk. It needs a purpose-built eval: questions whose answers changed between versions, graded against a stated as-of date."

"We don't have budget for a 550-query golden set. What now?"

"Then I'd reduce variance rather than add samples, because it's free and it's worth the same. Fix the query set across runs, fix seeds, change exactly one thing at a time — a paired design already captures most of the correlation between systems, and an unpaired test would need several times the sample.

Second, I'd measure a lower-variance quantity where I can. recall@N has much less per-query variance than nDCG@5 because it's insensitive to ordering, so if the change is a retrieval change, I'd measure retrieval.

Third — and this is the part that needs saying out loud — I'd report a directional read with the interval instead of a point estimate. 'B is probably better, CI minus 0.01 to plus 0.07, we shipped it because it's also simpler' is an honest engineering decision. 'B is 3% better' from 50 queries is a false claim, and I'd rather be the person who didn't make it.

I'd be cautious about using an LLM judge to expand the set. It buys sample size at the cost of label quality, and judge bias is systematic rather than random, so it doesn't average out — you can end up more confidently wrong with 10,000 judged queries than with 50 human-labelled ones. If we do it, we calibrate against a human subset and report the agreement rate next to every number."

The honest summary of this week
Four of the five decisions in ADR-003 rest on one measurement we have not yet made properly — the answer-span distribution. The arithmetic around it is sound and the sensitivity is clear, but a model is only as good as the number you feed it.

That's worth saying in an interview too. "Here is the model, here is what it recommends, and here is the input I'd want more evidence for before spending the money" is a stronger position than a confident recommendation with an unexamined premise underneath it.
