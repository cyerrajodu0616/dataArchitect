Chunking Strategies — The Decision Made Before Retrieval Exists

Week 3, Day 1 — chunk size as a joint quality and cost decision, with the arithmetic

Week 2 ended by naming eight questions that determine a retrieval architecture. Question 2 was chunks per document, and it was flagged as a decision, not a fact — one with a 2–4× infrastructure multiplier, settled before a single row is indexed. This lesson pays that off.

Two claims from Week 2 need refining, and both refinements make the decision more interesting:

What today changes about Week 2's answer

1. Chunk size moves two costs in opposite directions. Smaller chunks mean more vectors (index cost up) but fewer tokens per retrieved chunk (generation cost down). Total cost is U-shaped, and an index-only cost model points you at the wrong end of the curve.

2. "Smaller chunks are more precise" is true and incomplete. Precision rises as 1/C, but the probability that an answer survives intact falls. Below the length of a typical answer, no chunk can contain the answer at all — and no reranker recovers a fact that was cut in half at ingest.

1. A Chunk Serves Two Masters, and They Want Different Sizes

This is the tension underneath every chunking argument, and it is rarely stated. A chunk is both:

As a retrieval unitAs a generation unit
What it wants Small. One embedding must represent the whole chunk. Pack four topics into one vector and it lands in the average of four places — near none of them. Large. The model needs the clause and its exception, the figure and its caption, the rule and the section it sits under.
What goes wrong Big chunks blur. The embedding is a centroid, and a centroid of unrelated sentences is a poor match for any of them. Small chunks truncate. You retrieve a true sentence with its qualifier missing — a confidently wrong answer, which is worse than no answer.
Cost signal More chunks → bigger index → more RAM. More tokens per chunk → longer prompts → a bill that scales with traffic, not corpus.

Most teams resolve this by picking a compromise size and living with both problems. The better resolution is to stop making one object do both jobs — that is §9, and it is the single highest-leverage idea in this lesson. But the compromise is worth costing first, because it is what you will inherit.

2. The Geometry: When Does an Answer Survive Chunking?

Here is the part that is usually hand-waved as "overlap preserves context." It is derivable, and the derivation gives you an actual threshold to design against.

Let the answer be a contiguous span of L tokens. Chunks are C tokens with overlap O, so chunk i covers [iS, iS+C) where the stride is S = C − O. The span starting at p is fully inside chunk i exactly when iS ≤ p and p + L ≤ iS + C — that is, iS ≤ p ≤ iS + C − L. So of every S positions, min(S, C−L) are safe:

P(answer survives) = 1   if O ≥ L
P(answer survives) = (C − L) / (C − O)   if O < L < C
P(answer survives) = 0   if L ≥ C
The rule that falls out
Overlap at least as long as a typical answer span guarantees the answer is never split. Not "overlap helps" — a threshold, checkable against your own data. And the corollary matters just as much: if your chunk is shorter than the answer, no amount of overlap, reranking, or prompt engineering will fix it. The information was destroyed at ingest.

The second curve: signal density

Containment is only half of it. The other half is how much of the retrieved chunk is actually the answer — L/C. Everything else is noise you pay for twice: once in index RAM, and again in every prompt that retrieves it.

5. Buy Containment With Overlap, Not With Chunk Size

There are two ways to stop splitting answers, and they are priced completely differently:

LeverWhat it costsPaidTypical magnitude
Raise chunk size More tokens in every prompt that retrieves the chunk. On every query, forever Linear in C, multiplied by traffic.
Raise overlap Every token stored C/(C−O) times — more vectors, more index RAM. Once, at ingest 1.2× at 15% overlap; 2.5× at 60%.

Since generation dominates by 20–80× in every domain here, overlap is almost always the cheaper way to buy the guarantee. The sweep finds this without being told — it searches both axes:

python chunk_cost_sweep.py --domain insurance RECOMMENDATION Chunk size : 512 tokens, overlap 307 (60%) P(span intact) : 100.0% (SLO 95%) Signal density : 58.6% Cost : $984/mo (infra $201 + embed $1 + generation $781) Holding overlap at 15% and buying containment with CHUNK SIZE instead would mean 1600-token chunks at $1,927/mo — 2.0x more expensive for the same guarantee, because every extra token rides along in every prompt.
The result, and why it is not the usual advice
512-token chunks with 60% overlap beat 1,600-token chunks with 15% overlap — same containment guarantee, half the cost, and signal density of 58.6% instead of 18.8%. Conventional guidance ("10–20% overlap") is a default that was never derived from anything; it is right for short answer spans and badly wrong for long ones. Overlap should be set from your answer-span length, not from a blog post.

6. The Four Strategies

StrategyHow it worksAdvantagesDisadvantagesChoose it when
Fixed-size Hard cut every N tokens, optionally with overlap. Trivial, fast, perfectly predictable chunk count and cost. Splits mid-sentence and mid-word. Quality is not monotonic in chunk size — see the caution below. Baselines and cost modelling. Rarely the production answer.
Recursive Split on the largest separator that fits, backing off \n\n → \n → ". " → " ". Respects natural boundaries where they exist; degrades gracefully when they don't. Cheap. Boundary quality depends entirely on the separator list. No awareness of meaning or hierarchy. The default. Start here for anything unstructured.
Sentence / semantic Sentence: pack whole sentences to a budget. Semantic: embed each sentence, cut where consecutive-sentence distance exceeds a percentile. Never splits a sentence. Semantic finds real topic shifts a separator list can't see. Semantic costs an embedding pass over every sentence at ingest, and the threshold is another hyperparameter. Published gains over good recursive splitting are often small. Long-form prose with topic drift and no headings — transcripts, articles.
Structure-aware Split on the document's own hierarchy (headings, sections, clauses), pack within a section, and stamp the section path onto every chunk. Boundaries the author already chose, plus context that would otherwise be 400 tokens away. Requires documents that have structure, and a parser per format. Policy forms, regulatory text, API docs, contracts — anything filed or templated.

What the comparison actually shows

Why structural wins — and it is not better boundaries
Every structural chunk carries its section path, so "suicide, self-inflicted injury, or any cause excluded under Section 4.2" arrives labelled ACCIDENTAL DEATH BENEFIT RIDER instead of arriving as an anonymous paragraph about suicide. The embedding changes, retrieval changes, and the model can tell what it is reading.

This is also the cheapest possible version of contextual retrieval: you are prepending context the document already gave you, for free, at ingest. The expensive version — an LLM writing a bespoke summary for every chunk — buys more, but start here. A document with headings has already done most of the work and the usual pipeline throws it away.

Read the FIXED row again
It scores 3/3 at 90 tokens and drops back to 2/3 at 120. Quality is not monotonic in chunk size for a chunker that ignores the document: a bigger chunk moves every boundary, and an alignment that happened to work stops working.

That is not a tuning curve you can climb — it is luck. It is also why "we tuned chunk size on our eval set" can quietly mean "we overfit to which sentences happened to land together." If your chunk-size sweep is noisy and non-monotonic, that is a signal your chunker is fighting the document, not a signal to sweep harder.

How semantic chunking actually works, and when it is worth the ingest cost

Embed every sentence. Walk consecutive pairs and compute distance. Where the distance exceeds some percentile (commonly the 95th) of all pair distances in the document, declare a topic boundary. Then pack sentences between boundaries up to your budget.

The costs nobody mentions: an embedding call per sentence at ingest (for a 3.2B-token corpus that is real money and real wall-clock); a percentile threshold that behaves differently on a 2-page memo than on a 200-page filing; and non-deterministic chunk counts, which makes cost planning and re-ingest diffs harder.

When it earns its place: long-form prose with genuine topic drift and no headings to exploit — call transcripts, research reports, support threads. If your documents have headings, structure-aware chunking gets you most of the benefit for none of the cost.

The interview framing: "I'd use semantic chunking where the document has no structure to exploit, and I'd measure it against recursive splitting before adopting it — the published gains are real but modest, and it adds an embedding pass to ingest plus a threshold to maintain."

7. Overlap Is Not Free in Retrieval Either

The storage multiplier is the obvious cost. The subtle one: overlapping chunks are near-duplicates, and near-duplicates compete for your top-k slots. With 60% overlap, the three highest-scoring chunks for a query are frequently three windows over the same passage — so a nominal k=6 delivers the diversity of k=2, and the other relevant passage never makes the cut.

Two fixes, both cheap:

FixHowNote
Deduplicate by source span After retrieval, drop any chunk whose character range overlaps an already-selected chunk from the same document by more than ~50%. Retrieve k × 2 and dedupe down to k. Needs doc_id, start_char, end_char on every chunk — store them at ingest.
Merge adjacent survivors If two retrieved chunks are contiguous or overlapping in the same document, stitch them into one passage before prompting. Cuts duplicated tokens out of the prompt and repairs any span the boundary split. Strictly better than sending both.
This is why high overlap needs deduplication to be safe
The 60% overlap recommended for insurance in §5 is the right call on containment and cost — but only with span-dedup in the retrieval path. Without it you have bought answer survival and paid for it in result diversity, which is a bad trade you will not notice in an aggregate metric. Ship the two together or neither.

8. The Metadata That Makes a Chunk Usable

A chunk is not a string. It is a string plus the fields that let you filter, cite, dedupe, and reproduce it — and Week 2 established that filter selectivity is the load-bearing architecture question, which means this table is a capacity decision, not bookkeeping.

FieldWhyDomain that forces it
doc_id, chunk_ixParent lookup, adjacency, small-to-big (§9).all

section_pathThe context the heading carried. Prepended to the text and stored for filtering.insurance
doc_version, effective_from, effective_toBitemporal retrieval: "what did the policy say on the date of the claim." Week 2 Day 1 built this schema.insurance
jurisdiction / stateState form variation. Retrieving the Texas endorsement for a Colorado policy is a filed-form error.insurance

content_hash is the one people leave out and regret
Every chunking change is a full re-embed unless you can tell which chunks actually changed. With a content hash, editing one clause in one policy re-embeds a handful of chunks instead of 820,000. That converts "we can't change the chunking strategy, it's too expensive" — a sentence you will hear — into a routine deploy.

9. Small-to-Big: Stop Making One Object Do Both Jobs

Back to §1. The tension exists only because we assumed the retrieved unit and the generated-over unit are the same object. They don't have to be.

The implementation is unglamorous, which is the point — the parent lookup is a b-tree point read on doc_id, not a second vector search. You already store the parent text; the child chunks are just an index over it.

-- children carry the vector; parents carry the text CREATE TABLE parent_chunk ( parent_id bigserial PRIMARY KEY, doc_id bigint NOT NULL, section_path text, body text NOT NULL -- ~1600 tokens, no embedding ); CREATE TABLE child_chunk ( child_id bigserial PRIMARY KEY, parent_id bigint NOT NULL REFERENCES parent_chunk, start_char int NOT NULL, end_char int NOT NULL, body text NOT NULL, -- ~200 tokens embedding vector(1536) NOT NULL -- only the CHILD is indexed ); CREATE INDEX ON child_chunk USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops); CREATE INDEX ON child_chunk (parent_id); -- the "big" half of small-to-big
Where small-to-big stops helping
If several retrieved children map to the same parent, you have spent k retrieval slots to fetch one passage — the §7 diversity problem wearing a different hat. Dedupe parents before prompting and backfill the freed slots from the next-ranked children. And if parents are large, you have re-created the generation cost you avoided by indexing small: measure the prompt, don't assume it shrank.

10. How to Actually Choose — and What to Measure

The sweep gives you a starting point from geometry. It cannot tell you whether your answer spans are 40 or 300 tokens, and that single input dominates the recommendation. So the first task is not tuning — it is measurement.

#StepWhy this order
1Measure your answer-span length. Take 50 real questions, find the passage that answers each, and record its token length. Use the 90th percentile, not the mean.It sets both the overlap floor and the chunk-size floor. Everything downstream is guesswork without it. p90, because the tail is where confident-wrong answers come from.
2Set overlap ≥ p90 answer span.Buys containment at storage cost instead of prompt cost (§5).
3Take the smallest chunk size above that which your cost model tolerates.Signal density falls as 1/C; you pay for noise in RAM and in every prompt.
4Use structure where it exists, recursive where it doesn't.Free context, and robust to chunk budget (§6).
5Evaluate per query class, not in aggregate.Week 2 Day 4's finding, and it applies here with force — see below.
Step 5 is where chunk-size evaluations usually go wrong
Week 2 Day 4 showed an aggregate nDCG improving (+0.049) while the revenue-weighted number went down (−0.009). Chunk size is worse, because it affects query classes in opposite directions: small chunks help pinpoint lookups ("what's the return window") and hurt synthesis questions ("how does this rider interact with the base policy"). An aggregate score averages a win and a loss into a shrug.

Report chunk-size sweeps per class, always. If small chunks win on lookup and lose on synthesis — which is the usual pattern — that is not a tie, it is an argument for routing or for small-to-big, and the aggregate hid it.
