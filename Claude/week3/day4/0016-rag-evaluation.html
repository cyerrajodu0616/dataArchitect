RAG Evaluation — Metrics, Golden Sets, and the Statistics Nobody Runs

Week 3, Day 4 — measuring retrieval and generation, and knowing when your eval set can't

Days 1–3 produced three decisions that all need measuring: chunk size and overlap, hybrid arm weighting, and reranker plus candidate N. Each was justified with arithmetic and each ends with the same sentence — measure it on a golden set. Today is that.

The bulk of this lesson is not the metric definitions. Those take twenty minutes. It is the part that comes after, which is skipped almost universally:

The claim this lesson is built around
"B scored 0.03 higher" is not a result. It is a measurement whose error bars you have not computed. A typical 40–50 query golden set cannot resolve anything smaller than roughly 0.06–0.08 nDCG — which is larger than most real chunking and reranking gains. So the standard procedure (run the golden set against A and B, compare means, ship the winner) is frequently incapable of distinguishing the two systems, and reports a number anyway.

1. Two Systems, Measured Separately

A RAG pipeline fails in two independent places, and conflating them is the most common evaluation mistake:

RetrievalGeneration
QuestionDid the right information reach the prompt?Given what reached the prompt, was the answer faithful and useful?
Fails asThe answer isn't in the context at all.The answer is in the context and the model ignored it, contradicted it, or invented an addition.
FixChunking, hybrid weights, N, reranking — Days 1–3.Prompt, grounding instructions, citation requirements, model choice.
Measured withrecall@N, nDCG@k, MRR, precision@kGroundedness, answer relevance, completeness
Why an end-to-end score alone is close to useless
"Our RAG system scores 0.71" tells you nothing you can act on. Retrieval at 0.95 with generation at 0.75 and retrieval at 0.75 with generation at 0.95 produce similar end-to-end numbers and demand completely different work — one is a chunking and ranking problem, the other is a prompting problem. Always report both, always separately. Then the end-to-end number is a summary rather than a mystery.

2. Retrieval Metrics — Which, and When

Four metrics, and the discipline is knowing which question each answers:

MetricWhat it measuresUse it whenWhat it is blind to
recall@N Did the candidate set contain the answer at all? Always. It's Day 3's ceiling — no downstream stage can exceed it. Ordering entirely. recall@50 is unchanged whether the answer is at rank 1 or rank 50.
nDCG@k Graded, position-weighted quality of the final ranking. The user-facing ranking. It's the one to optimise for the top-k that enters the prompt. Anything outside k. It cannot see a retrieval failure — a query with no relevant doc anywhere scores 0 the same as one ranked badly.
MRR Reciprocal rank of the first relevant result. When there is exactly one correct answer — an ID lookup, a specific policy clause. All the other relevant documents. Wrong metric for "show me the options".
precision@k What fraction of what you sent was relevant. When the prompt budget is tight — Day 1 showed every irrelevant chunk is paid for twice. Whether the important one was included at all.
python rag_eval_harness.py retrieved (ranked) : [11, 4, 7, 2, 9] relevant : [4, 9, 15] graded {4: 3.0, 9: 2.0, 15: 3.0} recall@5 = 0.667 found 2 of 3 relevant documents precision@5 = 0.400 2 of the 5 returned were relevant MRR = 0.500 first relevant hit was at position 2 nDCG@5 = 0.453 position-weighted, graded, normalised

A team reporting only nDCG@5 cannot distinguish a retrieval failure from a ranking failure, and those have entirely different fixes. Report recall@N alongside it, always.

3. Generation Metrics — Groundedness Is the One That Matters

MetricDefinitionHow to compute it
Groundedness
(faithfulness)		 What fraction of the claims in the answer are supported by the retrieved context? Decompose the answer into atomic claims, then check each against the context. Claim-level, not answer-level — an answer that is 90% correct with one invented number is a failure, and an answer-level score of 0.9 hides that.
Answer relevance Does the answer address the question that was asked? Cheap proxy: have a model generate questions from the answer and measure similarity to the original. Catches the confident non-answer.
Completeness Did it use everything relevant that was in the context? The one people skip. Distinguishes "retrieval didn't find it" from "retrieval found it and the model ignored it" — completely different fixes.
Refusal correctness When the context doesn't contain the answer, does it say so? Feed queries whose answers are deliberately absent. Measure how often it declines instead of confabulating.
Refusal correctness deserves its own eval set
It is the metric that separates a demo from a production system, and it needs negative examples that no other part of the suite contains. Take 30 real questions whose answers you have deliberately removed from the corpus, and measure how often the system says "I don't know" versus how often it produces something plausible.

For insurance and fintech this is not a nice-to-have. A confident wrong answer about a suicide exclusion or a chargeback deadline is materially worse than no answer, and a suite of only-answerable questions cannot detect that failure mode at all. It also ties directly to Day 2 §5: pure RRF has no way to express "nothing good here", which is why the pre-fusion score floor was worth building.

Look at the per-class rows again
Every class is inconclusive, and the class sample sizes are 11, 22, 10 and 7. Per-class slicing is necessary — Week 2 Day 4 proved aggregates hide regressions — and it simultaneously multiplies your noise problem, because each slice holds a fraction of the sample. Those two facts are both true and they pull in opposite directions.

The resolution isn't to stop slicing. It's to size the eval set for the slice you care about most, not for the total. If NATURAL_LANGUAGE is 41% of traffic and you need to resolve +0.03 there, you need roughly 2.4× as many total queries as the aggregate calculation suggests.

6. How Many Queries Do You Actually Need?

This calculation takes two minutes and is almost never run. It should be run before labelling, because labelling is the expensive part and the only part whose cost you control up front.

MDE = (zα/2 + zβ) · SD / √n   ≈   2.80 · SD / √n

The one input is SD, the standard deviation of the per-query paired difference. And its size is the thing people get wrong:

Why the noise is larger than you'd guess
A retrieval change doesn't nudge every query slightly. It leaves most queries untouched and flips a minority hard — a query either surfaces the right chunk or it doesn't. So the per-query difference is a spike at zero plus a wide tail, and its SD lands around 0.16 rather than the 0.02–0.05 a "small noise" intuition suggests. That difference is an order of magnitude in required sample size, since n scales as SD².

Set against Day 3: a reranker's realistic contribution is often in the +0.02 to +0.05 range. So the eval set needed to justify the reranker is several hundred queries, and the one most teams have is fifty. That's not a rounding error — it means the reranker decision was made on noise.

7. When You Cannot Afford the Queries

Labelling 500 graded queries is real work. Four honest options, roughly in order of value:

#OptionWhat it costs you
1 Reduce variance instead of adding samples. Fix the query set across runs, fix seeds, change exactly one thing. Pairing already does a lot of this. Nothing. Variance removed is worth the same as sample added, and it is free. Do this first, always.
2 Measure a lower-variance quantity. recall@N has far less per-query variance than nDCG@5 because it's insensitive to ordering. You measure retrieval, not ranking — which is the right trade when the change is a retrieval change.
3 Accept a directional read, and say so. "B is probably better, CI [−0.01, +0.07]; we shipped it because it's also simpler." Nothing but the illusion of precision. This is an honest engineering decision. "B is 3% better" is a false claim.
4 Use an LLM judge to expand the set — carefully. Label quality. And judge bias is systematic, not random, so it does not average out with more samples — 10,000 judged queries can be more confidently wrong than 50 human-labelled ones. Calibrate against a human-labelled subset and report the agreement rate alongside every number.
The failure to avoid entirely
Running the same 40 queries a hundred times while tuning. That is not evaluation, it is optimisation against the sample. Every configuration you try gets another chance to look good by luck, and the gains you find will not survive contact with traffic. If you're going to iterate, hold out a set you look at once — and treat the number it gives you as the real one.

8. Three Domains, Three Different Suites

Retail

Primary metric: revenue-weighted nDCG@8.

Week 2 Day 4's trap: traffic-weighted nDCG rose +0.049 while revenue-weighted fell −0.009. High-traffic queries are not high-value queries.

Golden set from logs, stratified by class and weighted by margin. Plenty of traffic, so sample size is the cheap part here.

Life insurance

Primary metric: groundedness, then refusal correctness.

A confident wrong answer about an exclusion is a compliance event. Ranking quality is secondary to never asserting an unsupported claim.

The hard part: 20 QPS means few logged queries, so the golden set must be built by underwriters — expensive, and the power calculation matters most exactly where queries are scarcest.

Fintech

Primary metric: exact-match accuracy on codes and deadlines.

"Reason code 4853" versus "4837" is a binary outcome, not a graded one. nDCG is the wrong shape; report accuracy and MRR.

Negative set is mandatory: queries about codes that don't exist, to catch the plausible-neighbour failure from Day 2 §3.
