Reranking — The Ceiling, The Bill, and Where They Cross

Week 3, Day 3 — why a reranker cannot fix recall, and how to pick N without overpaying

A reranker is a second-stage model that re-scores the candidates the first stage returned. It is the highest-leverage single addition to most RAG pipelines, and it is also the component most often added for the wrong reason.

The sentence that settles most reranking arguments
A reranker improves precision. It cannot improve recall, ever, because it only sees what it was handed. Your end-to-end quality is bounded above by recall@N of stage one. If recall@100 = 0.72, then 28% of queries are unanswerable no matter how good the reranker is — and the reranker will spend real money reordering wrong answers.

1. Why a Second Stage Can Know More Than the First

This is not "a bigger model is better." It is a structural difference in what the model is allowed to look at.

Bi-encoder (retrieval)Cross-encoder (reranking)
Input Query and document embedded separately, never in the same forward pass. Query and document concatenated into one input. Attention runs across both.
What it can represent A fixed vector per document, computed before your query existed. It must be a good summary for every possible query. A score for this pair. It can notice that the query's "not" negates the document's claim, or that a number matches.
Cost Documents embedded once at ingest. Query cost is one forward pass, independent of corpus size. N forward passes per query. Nothing can be precomputed — the pair didn't exist until the query arrived.
Scales to Millions to billions of documents. Tens to hundreds of candidates.

That asymmetry is the architecture. You cannot cross-encode 4M products, and you don't have to — you only need to reorder the handful the first stage already narrowed to.

The consequence people miss
Because a bi-encoder embedding is computed before the query exists, it must be a compromise across all possible queries about that document. That is the same tension as Day 1 §1 — one object serving two masters — and it is why reranking helps even when retrieval is "working". The first stage isn't wrong; it's answering a harder question than the second stage is.

2. The Funnel, and the Ceiling It Imposes

achievable quality ≤ recall@N     prize = recall@N − recall@k

Two numbers, both cheap to compute, and between them they tell you whether to build a reranker at all:

What you measureWhat it tells youWhat to do
recall@N is low (< 0.90)The answer often isn't in the candidate set.Stop. Fix retrieval — chunking, hybrid weights, query expansion. A reranker reorders wrong answers.
recall@k ≈ recall@NThe first stage already puts the answer in the top k.Stop. There is no headroom; a reranker is pure latency and cost.
Large gap between themThe answer is being retrieved but ranked badly.Rerank. This is exactly the situation the tool exists for.

3. Cost Is Linear. Quality Saturates. Therefore There Is an Optimum.

This is the part that decides N, and it follows from two facts that are true of every reranker:

  • Cost is linear in N. N candidates means N forward passes. Double N, double the GPU fleet.
  • The recall curve saturates. Going from 10 to 50 candidates recovers a lot; going from 500 to 1000 recovers almost nothing.

A linear cost against a saturating benefit guarantees an efficiency optimum strictly below whatever your latency budget allows. Which means the common decision procedure — "set N to whatever fits the latency budget" — is reliably wrong, and reliably expensive.

4. Reranker Options

OptionThroughputAdvantagesDisadvantagesChoose when
MiniLM cross-encoder (6-layer) ~2,000 pairs/s on an L4 Most of the quality of a bigger model at a quarter of the compute. Small enough to co-locate. Short input limit; long chunks get truncated, which silently discards the end of a clause. The default. Start here.
Base cross-encoder (12-layer, 512 tok) ~500 pairs/s Better ordering, handles longer chunks without truncation. 4× the compute for a modest gain. At high QPS that is a 4× fleet. Low QPS, long chunks, high miss cost.
Listwise LLM Bounded by prompt length Best quality available. Sees all candidates together, so it can reason comparatively rather than scoring pairs in isolation. Latency scales with N × chunk tokens. See the reality check below. Offline evaluation, golden-set labelling, or genuinely low-volume high-value queries.
ColBERT / late interaction Between the two Per-token embeddings with interaction at query time — much of the cross-encoder's benefit, precomputable. Storage explodes: one vector per token, not per chunk. That's a Week 2 capacity problem all over again. You need cross-encoder quality at bi-encoder latency and can pay in storage.

The LLM reranker reality check

"We have a 2-second latency budget, so we can afford an LLM reranker" sounds reasonable and is wrong. Run it:

python rerank_economics.py --domain insurance --reranker llm N recall@N headroom rerank ms total ms fits? $/month $ per +0.01 ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ 10 0.620 +0.022 1280.0 1340.0 yes 16,589 7,707 25 0.691 +0.092 3200.0 3260.0 NO 41,472 4,485 50 0.781 +0.182 6400.0 6460.0 NO 82,944 4,545 100 0.890 +0.292 12800.0 12860.0 NO 165,888 5,690
A generous latency budget does not imply an LLM reranker fits
At 512-token chunks, even N=25 blows a 2-second budget — the binding constraint is prompt length, not QPS. And N=10 costs $16,589/month against $584 for a cross-encoder at N=100, which is 28× the price for a worse ceiling (0.620 vs 0.890), because it can only afford to look at 10 candidates.

This is the general shape of the LLM-reranker argument: it's the best reranker per candidate and the worst per dollar, so it wins only where N can be small — which is where you needed it least. Its real home is offline: labelling golden sets and evaluating other rerankers, where latency doesn't exist and quality is the whole point.

5. Rerank Only What Needs It

Retail's $7,592/month assumes reranking every query. It shouldn't. Week 2 Day 4 built a query-class router; this is its second job.

Query classRerank?Why
EXACT_ID — SKU, form number, reason codeNoThe lexical arm either found it or it doesn't exist. There is nothing to reorder, and headroom is ~0.
NAVIGATIONAL — brand or category browseNoOrdering is driven by business rules — stock, margin, popularity — not semantic relevance.
NATURAL_LANGUAGE — questions, descriptionsYesExactly the class with a large recall@k → recall@N gap. All of the headroom lives here.
AMBIGUOUS — short, underspecifiedYesFirst-stage ordering is close to arbitrary; the reranker has the most to add.
What routing is worth here
If 40% of retail traffic is EXACT_ID or NAVIGATIONAL, routing drops the fleet from 13 GPUs to 8 — $7,592 → $4,672/month, a 38% saving — with no quality loss, because those classes had no headroom to begin with. That is a better return than any amount of model tuning, and it reuses a component you already built.

It is also the honest answer to "reranking is too expensive at our QPS": usually it is too expensive applied indiscriminately, and affordable applied where it works.

6. Three Domains, Three Different Calls

Retail

500 QPS · 150 ms
r@10 0.71 · r@100 0.93

N=50, MiniLM, routed. $7,592/mo unrouted, $4,672 routed.

QPS is the binding constraint, not quality. The reranker costs 8× the entire vector index.

This is an economics problem before it is a relevance problem.

Life insurance

20 QPS · 2,000 ms
r@10 0.62 · r@100 0.89

N=100, MiniLM. $584/mo, $20 per +0.01 — the cheapest quality on the page.

27 points of headroom between r@10 and r@100 sitting unused. Textbook case for reranking.

But r@100 = 0.89 → 11% unanswerable. Fix retrieval too.

Fintech documents

30 QPS · 1,500 ms
r@10 0.68 · r@100 0.91

N=50, MiniLM. $584/mo, $37 per +0.01.

One GPU with room to spare — below one machine's capacity, more candidates are free at the margin.

The decision is which model, not whether.

The GPU-granularity effect worth knowing
Insurance costs the same $584/month at N=10 and at N=100, because both fit inside one GPU. Below a machine's capacity, additional candidates are free at the margin — so the efficiency argument in §3 inverts and you should take the largest N that still fits one machine. Check where your step function is before optimising against a smooth curve that isn't there.

7. The Order to Do Things In

#StepWhy this order
1Measure recall@N of stage one.It's the ceiling, it costs nothing to compute, and you already have the golden set from Week 2 Day 5.
2If recall@100 < 0.90 — stop and fix retrieval.Chunking (Day 1), hybrid weighting (Day 2), query expansion. A reranker on a candidate set without the answer reorders wrong answers at full price.
3If recall@k ≈ recall@N — stop.No headroom. You'd be buying latency.
4Pick N on cost per unit of ceiling.Linear cost against a saturating curve means the optimum is below your latency limit, not at it.
5Route: rerank only the classes with headroom.Typically ~38% off the bill for zero quality loss.
6Measure the realised gain, per class.The ceiling is arithmetic; the gain is empirical. That's Day 4.
The interview answer
"Reranking improves precision, never recall — so the first thing I'd measure is recall@N of the first stage, because that's a hard ceiling on anything the reranker can achieve. If recall@100 is below about 0.90 I'd fix retrieval before adding a second stage at all. If there's a real gap between recall@k and recall@N, that gap is the prize and reranking is the right tool. I'd size N on cost per unit of recall rather than on what fits the latency budget — cost is linear in N and the recall curve saturates, so the efficient point is always below the limit. On our retail numbers that's N=50 at about $520 per point of ceiling, versus $1,550 at N=200, which is what 'make it fit' would have picked. Then I'd route, so we only rerank the classes that have headroom — exact-ID lookups have none."
